## Lesson 4.1: The Lifecycle at a Glance

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can name the seven stages of a public procurement, and map them onto the five-stage cycle from Module 1.

### Chunk 1: From five stages to seven

Module 1 gave you the universal five-stage cycle. Public buyers break the same journey into more steps, because each step needs its own documentation and approval.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[1. Requirement] --> B[2. Planning and Budget]
    B --> C[3. Solicitation]
    C --> D[4. Evaluation]
    D --> E[5. Award]
    E --> F[6. Contract Administration]
    F --> G[7. Closeout]
```

| Module 1 stage | Public lifecycle stages |
|---|---|
| Identify the need | 1. Requirement, 2. Planning and Budget |
| Source and compare | 3. Solicitation, 4. Evaluation |
| Award and contract | 5. Award |
| Deliver and receive | 6. Contract Administration |
| Pay and close out | 6. Contract Administration, 7. Closeout |

### Chunk 2: Three phases

The seven stages are easier to hold in mind when you group them into three phases. The first, pre-solicitation (stages 1 and 2), is where the buyer decides what it needs and whether it can pay for it, and most of a procurement's success is decided here. The second, solicitation to award (stages 3 to 5), is the competitive part, in which suppliers are invited, compared, and one is selected. The third, post-award (stages 6 and 7), covers delivery, oversight, payment, and wrapping up.

> **Varies by jurisdiction:** Names and number of stages differ. Some entities fold planning into the requirement, and others treat award and contracting as separate steps, so it's the sequence that matters more than the labels.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Which Module 1 stage covers "Source and compare" in the public lifecycle?
> 2. A new rep says the competitive bidding phase is where most procurements are won or lost. Which phase would you point them to instead, and why?

> **Takeaway**
> The public lifecycle is the same journey as the five-stage cycle, broken into more documented steps.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> "Where are you in the process?" is a natural opening question, and the answer tells you what the buyer needs to hear from you.

## Lesson 4.2: Before the Solicitation: Need, Planning and Budget

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe what happens before a solicitation is published, and why the earliest stages matter most.

### Chunk 1: Requirement

Someone in a department defines the need: what is required, in what quantity, by when, and to what standard. In practice this often becomes a **scope of work** for services or a set of **specifications** for goods.

A weak requirement is the most common source of later trouble, since vague scope leads to mismatched bids, change orders, or disputes.

### Chunk 2: Planning and budget

Before spending anything, the buyer has several questions to settle. The first is whether the money is there, because funds must be budgeted or otherwise available, and they may be tied to a grant or a specific fund. The second is how much the purchase is likely to cost; an estimate, sometimes called an independent cost estimate, helps place the purchase in the right tier and gives the buyer something to judge bids against later.

The estimated value and the type of need then guide the choice of method, meaning a quote process or a formal competition (Module 3). Higher-value purchases may need sign-off before going out, so the buyer also works out who has to approve. Last, the buyer checks for an existing vehicle, since a cooperative or existing contract might make a new solicitation unnecessary.

### Chunk 3: Why this stage is quiet and powerful

Suppliers usually don't see these stages, yet many outcomes are shaped here: how the scope is written, which method is chosen, and what the budget allows.

> **Varies by jurisdiction:** Budget cycles, planning requirements, and approval steps differ widely. Many entities budget annually, so the timing of a purchase often follows the fiscal calendar.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is a scope of work?
> 2. A department asks for "a better scheduling system" and nothing more. What problems is that likely to cause later?
> 3. Why do many outcomes get decided before suppliers see anything?

> **Takeaway**
> Need and planning shape everything downstream, which is why a clear requirement and confirmed funding are the foundation of a clean procurement.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> The earlier you understand the buyer's need and timing, the more useful you can be, and learning their budget cycle often explains why the timing looks the way it does.

## Lesson 4.3: Solicitation, Evaluation and Award

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain how a public buyer invites, compares, and selects suppliers, and why the process is documented the way it is.

### Chunk 1: Solicitation

The buyer publishes a request that tells suppliers what is needed and how to respond. A solicitation typically includes the scope or specifications, the instructions for responding and the deadline, and the evaluation criteria that say how responses will be judged. It also carries the required forms and terms, along with a contact for questions and the rules on communication during the process.

Public notice is common so that suppliers have a fair chance to see it. Many buyers allow questions and answer them for everyone, often through an addendum, so that no one has an informational edge. Module 5 covers the types of solicitations in detail.

### Chunk 2: Evaluation

After the deadline, responses are reviewed in three steps.

1. **Compliance check:** Did the supplier follow the instructions and submit what was required?
2. **Evaluation against the stated criteria:** Some processes look at price alone, while others weigh price together with quality, experience, and approach.
3. **Documentation:** Scoring and notes are kept so that the decision can be explained later.

Throughout, buyers evaluate against the criteria they published and not against new ones, since changing the standard after responses arrive would undo the fairness the process exists to protect.

### Chunk 3: Award

The buyer selects the supplier according to the process. Depending on size and rules, the award may need approval from a manager, board, or council, and it may be announced publicly. After the award, the buyer and supplier finalize the contract.

Unsuccessful suppliers may be able to request a debrief or file a protest, depending on local rules, which is another reason the paper trail matters.

> **Varies by jurisdiction:** Rules for communication during a solicitation, public bid openings, debriefs, protest windows, and award announcements differ by entity.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Why do buyers often answer supplier questions for everyone, not just the person who asked?
> 2. Midway through scoring, a buyer wishes the criteria had weighted past experience more heavily. Why can't they simply change the weighting?
> 3. Name two reasons buyers document evaluation so carefully.

> **Takeaway**
> Solicitation, evaluation, and award form the visible competitive core of the lifecycle, and fairness and documentation drive how each step is run.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> During an open solicitation, follow the buyer's communication rules strictly, because contact outside the designated channel can disqualify a supplier or taint the process.

## Lesson 4.4: After the Award: Administration and Closeout

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe what happens after a contract is awarded and why this stage matters to both sides.

### Chunk 1: Contract administration

Awarding the contract isn't the end, because someone still has to make sure the agreement is carried out. That means monitoring performance against the contract terms and deliverables, and managing changes, which are usually made through formal modifications or amendments. It also means reviewing and approving invoices before paying them, handling issues such as delays, quality problems, or disputes, and tracking dates such as renewals and expirations.

The buyer's contract administrator or project manager is often a different person from the one who ran the solicitation.

### Chunk 2: Closeout

When the work or term is complete, the buyer accepts the final deliverables, pays the final invoices, and releases any retained amounts. Records are filed and retained as required, and performance may be documented. Those records often have to be kept for a set period, because audits and public records requests can come years later.

### Chunk 3: The loop back

How a supplier performs becomes part of the buyer's experience, and in some places part of a formal record, which then shapes future procurements. The lifecycle is a loop, much like the cycle in Module 1.

### Chunk 4: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Requirement** | The defined need that a purchase must satisfy |
| **Scope of work** | A description of the services and deliverables a buyer expects |
| **Specifications** | A description of the goods or technical standards a buyer expects |
| **Solicitation** | A published request that invites supplier responses |
| **Addendum** | A written change or clarification issued to all suppliers |
| **Evaluation criteria** | The published factors used to judge responses |
| **Award** | The selection of the supplier |
| **Contract administration** | The work of overseeing performance after award |
| **Modification / amendment** | A formal change to a contract |
| **Closeout** | The final stage, in which a contract is completed and documented |
| **Fiscal year** | The 12-month budgeting period an entity uses |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Why is contract administration often handled by a different person than the one who ran the solicitation?
> 2. A supplier wants to add extra deliverables partway through a contract, and the buyer agrees. What should happen so the change is valid?
> 3. Why are records kept after closeout?

> **Takeaway**
> The work isn't over at award, since administration and closeout determine whether the contract delivers and feed into the buyer's next decision.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> Many buyers care as much about what happens after award as before it, so knowing how they track performance and invoicing shows you understand their full lifecycle.
