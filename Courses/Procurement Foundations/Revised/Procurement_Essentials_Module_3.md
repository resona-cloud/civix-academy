## Lesson 3.1: Where the Rules Come From

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain the layers of rules a state, county, or local buyer follows, and why you must always ask which rules apply.

### Chunk 1: Layers of rules

A public buyer's process is usually shaped by several layers of rules at once.

| Layer | What it is | Example of what it sets |
|---|---|---|
| **State law** | Statutes passed by the legislature | Minimum competition requirements for local entities, public notice rules |
| **Local code or ordinance** | Rules adopted by a city, county, or district | Dollar tiers, approval levels, preferences |
| **Board or council policy** | Adopted policies and resolutions | Procedures, delegated authority |
| **Department procedures** | Internal guides and templates | Forms, checklists, who signs what |
| **Funding conditions** | Requirements attached to grants or special funds | Extra steps when outside money pays for the purchase |

When the layers disagree, the stricter one usually wins. A city may be free to set its own tiers, but only inside what state law allows, and grant money can add requirements on top of both.

### Chunk 2: Why this matters

Two neighboring entities can buy the same item under very different rules, because there's no single national rulebook for state and local buying. Public-sector buyers generally set their own tiers, dollar amounts, and approval levels, and that is the reason you ask which rules apply instead of assuming.

> **Varies by jurisdiction:** Everything in this module. The right habit is to ask, not assume.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three layers of rules a local buyer may follow.
> 2. A city's ordinance allows purchases up to a certain amount without bidding, but a state statute sets a lower limit for local governments. Which one governs, and why?

> **Takeaway**
> There's no single rulebook for state and local buying: state law, local code, policy, and funding conditions stack on top of one another, and the stricter layer governs.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> Ask, "What process do you follow for a purchase like this?" instead of telling them what the rules are. They know their own rules, and asking keeps you from misquoting ones that don't apply.

## Lesson 3.2: Dollar Thresholds and the Tiers of Process

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe the typical three-tier structure of public purchasing and explain why thresholds differ so much.

### Chunk 1: The typical tiers

Most public buyers scale the formality of the process to the size of the purchase.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Small purchase] --> B[Informal quotes]
    B --> C[Formal competition]
    A1[Little or no competition required, staff discretion] -.-> A
    B1[Several price quotes gathered] -.-> B
    C1[Public solicitation, scoring, award, board approval] -.-> C
```

| Tier | Typical process | Who decides |
|---|---|---|
| **Small purchase** | Often no bidding requirement; staff use judgment | Department or purchasing staff |
| **Informal / quote tier** | Staff typically gather several price quotes, sometimes with room to pick something other than the lowest price | Purchasing staff, sometimes a director |
| **Formal competition** | Published solicitation, market research, scoring, award, protest handling, and contract approval | Purchasing office plus approval by a manager, board, or council |

Formal competition is where much of the money goes. One practitioner overview says roughly 80% of local government spending falls in the formal tier, and that the process can take anywhere from several months to a couple of years. Both figures come from a single source, so treat them as rough context and not as a promise.

### Chunk 2: How much thresholds vary

A December 2024 comparison of state small-purchase thresholds found limits ranging from about **$1,000 to $250,000**, depending on the state. The figures measure different things (direct-purchase limits, quote requirements, and competition triggers), which means they can't be compared directly. Three illustrations from that comparison show the spread: one state requires only good purchasing practice below a few thousand dollars, another requires no competition up to $10,000 and three quotes from $10,000 to $25,000, and a third allows documented quotes for purchases up to $250,000.

City examples from a local-purchasing overview show the same spread, with informal ranges that start in the low thousands and, in some places, reach six figures.

> **Varies by jurisdiction:** These are examples of the spread, not figures to repeat. Thresholds change whenever laws and policies are amended, so figures like these go stale quickly. For an actual buyer, find their current policy.

### Chunk 3: Why the tiers exist

Heavy process costs time and money for both sides, so tiers aim to match the effort to the risk. A few hundred dollars of supplies doesn't need a formal bid, whereas a large multi-year contract does.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What are the three typical tiers of public purchasing?
> 2. A colleague wants to tell a county buyer, "You need a formal bid above $50,000, right?" because that was the limit at another county. What should they do instead?
> 3. Why do the tiers exist?

> **Takeaway**
> Process scales with the size of the purchase, but where the lines sit depends on the entity, and the lines move over time.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> Learn where the purchase sits relative to the buyer's thresholds, since a buyer under the formal threshold may be able to move quickly while one above it follows a longer, public process.

## Lesson 3.3: Exceptions: Sole Source, Emergency and Local Programs

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can recognize the common exceptions to competition and the programs that shape who gets a chance to compete.

### Chunk 1: Common exceptions

Competition is the default, but most jurisdictions allow narrow exceptions, each with its own documentation requirements.

| Exception | General idea | Typical safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| **Sole source** | Only one supplier can provide what is needed (for example, proprietary products or unique services) | Written justification, sometimes public notice or higher approval |
| **Emergency** | An urgent threat to health, safety, or essential services leaves no time for the normal process | Documentation after the fact, limits on scope and duration |
| **Existing contract use** | An already-competed contract can be used (covered in Lesson 3.4) | Must be permitted by local rules |

Exceptions are meant to be rare and defensible. A buyer who asks for a sole-source justification isn't being difficult; they're protecting themselves from audit findings.

### Chunk 2: Programs that shape the field

Many entities run programs that influence who competes or how bids are evaluated. Small, local, or disadvantaged business programs are one example, and they can include outreach requirements during informal bidding or allowances for purchases from certified firms. Some jurisdictions also use preferences or set-asides, and funding can add its own requirements when grants or special funds pay for the purchase.

> **Varies by jurisdiction:** Whether these programs exist, who qualifies, and how they work depends entirely on the entity and, often, on the funding source.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Give one reason a buyer might legitimately skip competition.
> 2. A buyer buys an urgent repair without bids and then spends an afternoon writing up what happened. Why do buyers document exceptions so carefully?

> **Takeaway**
> Competition is the default, and the exceptions to it are narrow and documented, while local programs can shape who gets to compete in the first place.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> Never suggest a way around the process. Buyers are expected to justify any exception, and being asked to bend the rules damages trust.

## Lesson 3.4: Cooperative Purchasing and Piggybacking

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain how public entities share contracts and why it matters to suppliers and buyers.

### Chunk 1: Sharing one competition

Cooperative purchasing lets public entities share a single competitive process rather than each running its own. It takes three common forms.

| Form | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Joint solicitation** | Several entities issue a shared bid, splitting the work and combining buying power |
| **Multiple-award schedules** | A lead agency (often a state) competitively awards contracts to several qualified suppliers; other agencies buy from those suppliers |
| **Piggybacking** | An entity uses a contract another entity already awarded competitively |

Purchasing cooperatives and regional bodies often coordinate these contracts, and suppliers commonly pay them administrative fees. NASPO ValuePoint, Sourcewell, and U.S. Communities are examples of such organizations.

### Chunk 2: Benefits and cautions

For buyers, the appeal is practical. A shared contract saves staff time, avoids a duplicate procurement, combines volume for better pricing, and lets a buyer rely on vetting that peers have already done. It can also speed access to new vendors and technologies.

The cautions are just as practical. Local rules still apply, so entities often adapt a shared contract to their own requirements. The legal authority to use cooperative contracts, or another entity's contracts, varies by state, which is why buyers check before relying on one. Cooperatives also differ in governance and fees, so their terms get reviewed as well.

### Chunk 3: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Threshold** | A dollar level at which the required process changes |
| **Informal quotes** | A lighter process in which staff gather several price quotes |
| **Formal competition** | A published, scored, and documented competitive process |
| **Sole source** | An award to one supplier because no competition is practical |
| **Emergency purchase** | An urgent purchase made through a streamlined process and documented afterward |
| **Cooperative purchasing** | An arrangement in which public entities share a competitively awarded contract |
| **Piggybacking** | A way of using another entity's already-competed contract |
| **Lead agency** | The entity that runs a shared solicitation |
| **Ordinance / code** | A set of local legal rules adopted by a city or county |
| **Delegated authority** | Approval power that a governing body gives to staff up to a set level |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two forms of cooperative purchasing.
> 2. A small town wants to buy through another city's contract but isn't sure its state allows it. What should the buyer do first, and why?

> **Takeaway**
> Shared contracts save buyers time, but they only work where local and state rules permit them.

> **When you're talking to a buyer**
> Asking whether they use cooperative contracts tells you whether their buying runs through a shared vehicle or through their own solicitations.
