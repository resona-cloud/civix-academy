# Sales Essentials
## Module 6: Objections

**Audience:** Everyone at Resona who talks with people about buying
**Prerequisite:** Sales Essentials Modules 1 to 5
**Lessons:** 6.1 What an Objection Really Is · 6.2 A Simple Process · 6.3 Common Objections · 6.4 Respecting a No
**Who "the customer" is:** Individual decision-makers, mostly small business owners and their staff.

## Lesson 6.1: What an Objection Really Is

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain what an objection is, see it as information and not an attack, and name common types.

### Chunk 1: Objections are normal and useful

An **objection** is a concern, question, or hesitation a person raises about buying, and it isn't rudeness. It usually turns out to be one of three things.

| It may be... | Example | What the person wants |
|---|---|---|
| **A question** | "How long does setup take?" | An answer |
| **A worry** | "What if my staff won't use it?" | Reassurance backed by facts |
| **A real no** | "We're not interested." | Respect |

People who aren't interested rarely bother raising concerns; they just stop answering. An objection therefore often means that the person is still engaged and needs something to decide.

### Chunk 2: Treat it as a question

An open textbook on selling (*Selling for Success*, NSCC) lists six habits for handling almost any objection. The first and most useful is to view the objection as a question and not as a personal attack. The other five are to respond with a question to understand it, restate the objection before answering, and pause before you respond. The textbook also recommends using real examples and testimonials where they fit, and never arguing with the person.

The textbook draws on practitioner and trade sources and not on studies, so it makes a good checklist but isn't proof.

### Chunk 3: Six common types

The same textbook groups objections into six categories, which make a good starting map.

| Type | What it sounds like | What is often underneath |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** | "Will it work for my kind of shop?" | A doubt about fit or risk |
| **Source** | "I've never heard of your company." | A question about trust |
| **Price** | "That's more than I expected." | A doubt about value, or habit |
| **Money** | "Cash is tight right now." | A real limit on affordability or timing |
| **Already satisfied** | "We're fine with what we have." | Possibly a hidden concern, or no felt need |
| **"I need to think about it"** | "Let me get back to you." | A worry not yet spoken, or a soft no |

### Chunk 4: Hidden objections

The spoken objection may not be the real one. "It's too expensive" can mean "I don't believe it will work," and "Let me think about it" can mean "I'm worried about something and I don't want to say." The next lesson shows how to ask kindly to find out.

### Chunk 5: What not to do

The textbook's cautions match common sense. Do not argue, call the person wrong, or get defensive, and do not criticize competitors or your own company or product. Do not lie: if you don't know, say so and follow up. And do not ignore an objection or talk over it. This course adds one more: do not use pressure to push past a concern.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three things an objection may be.
> 2. Why can an objection be a sign of engagement?
> 3. A customer asks, "What if my staff won't use it?" Is this a question, a worry, or a real no, and what do they need from you?

> **Takeaway**
> An objection gives you information about what the person still needs in order to decide, which is why it's best treated as a question to understand and not an attack to answer.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> When a concern comes up, pause briefly and restate it as a question, such as "So you're wondering whether your staff would actually use it?", before offering any answer.

## Lesson 6.2: A Simple Process

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can handle an objection using five steps: listen, acknowledge, explore, respond, and check.

### Chunk 1: The five steps

A common training model is called **LAER**: Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, Respond. Sales trainers teach it, and several consulting and training sites describe it, but none of them provide independent evidence that it works better than other approaches. It's a sensible structure, and this course adds a fifth step, **Check**, because an answer is only useful if it landed.

| Step | What you do | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Listen** | Let them finish. Do not interrupt. Notice tone and feeling. | (Silence, nodding) |
| **2. Acknowledge** | Show you heard it. This is not agreeing. | "That's a fair concern." |
| **3. Explore** | Ask an open question to find the real issue. | "Can you tell me more about what worries you?" |
| **4. Respond** | Answer what the exploring showed, truthfully and briefly. | "Here's how setup works for most shops." |
| **5. Check** | Ask whether it answered the concern. | "Does that address it, or is there more?" |

### Chunk 2: Why explore before answering

A common mistake in handling objections is to answer the words a customer used and not the concern behind them. When a customer says, "That's too much for us," the instinctive response is to defend the price, yet those few words can carry quite different meanings. The monthly amount may truly be beyond the budget, the customer may not yet be convinced the product will pay for itself, or the decision may rest with someone else whose objection the customer is anticipating. Each calls for a different reply, and from the outside there's no way to tell which one you're facing.

That's why exploring comes before responding. A question such as "When you say it's too much, is it the monthly amount, or are you not sure yet that it would pay off?" accomplishes two things at once: it shows the customer the comment was taken seriously, and it invites them to say what they meant.

> **Customer:** "That's too much for us."
> **Weak:** "But it saves you so much time!"
> **Better:** "Understood. When you say it's too much, is it the monthly cost, or are you not sure yet it would pay off?"

The weak reply isn't rude, but it answers a question the customer never asked. A consulting-site article on LAER gives the same caution, that confidence you already know the real concern deserves suspicion, though it cites no data, so it's best read as practical advice. If the concern turns out to be cost, that's one conversation, covered in the next lesson, whereas if it's doubt, you may need to show how the product works.

### Chunk 3: Useful phrases

| Purpose | Try |
|---|---|
| **Acknowledge** | "That makes sense." "I'm glad you said that." "A lot of owners ask that." |
| **Restate** | "So you're worried your team won't use it. Have I got that right?" |
| **Explore** | "What would you need to see to feel comfortable?" "What's behind that?" |
| **Respond** | "Here's what I know..." "Here's what I don't know yet, and I'll find out." |
| **Check** | "Is that helpful?" "What else is on your mind?" |

### Chunk 4: Keep the pace calm

Pausing before you answer helps, and the textbook advises it so that you don't oversell. Keeping your voice level matters too, since defensiveness sounds like pressure, and it's better not to rush to fill silence, because a pause after your answer often brings out the real concern. Handle one concern at a time, and ask "Is there anything else?" so that you don't discover a second concern after solving the first.

### Chunk 5: Honest answers only

These limits apply when you respond. You may not invent urgency ("This price ends today") unless it's a real, published end date, and you may not promise outcomes you can't prove. You may not pretend to know something you don't, and you may not minimize a real limit: if the concern is valid, say so.

A valid concern that the product cannot answer isn't a failure. It's the point where you may say, "This might not be right for you."

### Chunk 6: A short fictional exchange

> **Lena:** "I'm not sure my staff will actually use it."
> **Jordan:** "That's a fair worry. Can you tell me more about what's happened with new tools in the past?"
> **Lena:** "We tried an app once. Nobody opened it after a week."
> **Jordan:** "That's helpful to know. What made it hard to use?"
> **Lena:** "Too many steps."
> **Jordan:** "Then let me show you what a booking takes here, and you tell me if it's too many steps." *(demonstrates)* "How does that compare?"
> **Lena:** "That's simpler. I'd want my front desk to try it."

Jordan neither argued nor promised anything. Lena's concern was fear of repeating a bad experience, and showing her was the answer.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name the five steps.
> 2. A customer says, "That's too much for us." Why is it better to ask a question before defending the price?
> 3. What is one thing you may not do when responding?

> **Takeaway**
> Listening, acknowledging, exploring, responding, and checking keeps the answer tied to the person's real concern, and a calm, honest pace keeps it from sounding like pressure.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> When you hear an objection, say it back before you answer: "So your concern is ___. Is that right?"

## Lesson 6.3: Common Objections

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can respond honestly to price, money, trust, fit, "we're fine," and "I need to think about it" objections, and handle requests that involve someone else.

These are patterns and not scripts. Say them in your own words, and only say what is true.

### Chunk 1: Price

Fixed pricing changes this objection. Because pricing is set in advance as a subscription, there is nothing to negotiate, and you do not make up discounts or promise a different price. That makes the conversation simpler and more honest.

An open textbook says price objections are often a habit or a negotiating tactic, and that the response is to probe the concern and build the case for value and not to cut the price. With a fixed price, that's the only option anyway.

| What they say | What to do |
|---|---|
| "That's more than I expected." | Acknowledge. Ask what they expected and what they are comparing it to |
| "Can you do better on price?" | Be straight: "The price is the same for everyone. What I can do is make sure the plan fits what you need." |
| "Why is it this much?" | Explain what the plan includes and what it replaces, using their own numbers if they gave them |
| "Your competitor is cheaper." | Do not attack. Ask what is included, and compare fairly |

You may explain the published plans and point out a plan that fits their needs better, and you may mention official, published promotions if they apply. You may also be clear about what the price includes and doesn't include, and when it starts.

You may not invent a discount or a "special" price, promise a price change later, or imply that the price is negotiable when it is not.

### Chunk 2: Money and timing

"Cash is tight" and "not this quarter" are real concerns, and they shouldn't be argued with. Ask instead:

> "That makes sense. Is it more about the amount, or about when the money comes in?"

Then be honest about what the commitment is: the cost, when billing starts, how to cancel, and anything else they should know. If now isn't right, agree on a later date and keep it.

### Chunk 3: Trust and "I've never heard of you"

This is a fair concern about a company they don't know, and it deserves an honest answer. Say who Resona is in plain terms, without exaggerating size, history, or customer numbers. Offer proof that is real and permitted, and offer a way to try or verify if the company has one. Do not say "everyone knows us."

> "Fair question. We're a newer company, and I'd rather be upfront about that. Here's what we do and how you can check it out for yourself."

### Chunk 4: Product and fit

"Will it work for my business?" is a good question. Explore what they mean, show how it works in their situation, and say honestly what the product does not do. If it doesn't fit, say so, and don't stretch an example to make it seem like it does.

### Chunk 5: "We're fine with what we have" (already satisfied)

The textbook notes that this can be a hidden objection. Don't argue; ask with curiosity:

> "Good to hear. What do you like most about how it works now? Is there anything you wish were easier?"

Do not criticize their current tool or provider. If nothing is wrong, respect that, and offer to check back at a better time if they would like.

### Chunk 6: "I need to think about it"

The open textbook says this is usually a stall that hides a worry. A sales trainer's blog, which is practitioner opinion with no data, says it can be a "slow no" or a "pending yes" and advises finding out which. A practical approach has four parts. Agree kindly ("Of course. It's a decision worth thinking about"), then ask what they will be weighing ("What are you thinking through? Is there something I could clear up now?"). Offer a specific next step, such as a short follow-up on a date or a summary they can review, and do not pressure them: if they want time, give it.

If there is a hidden worry, a calm question often brings it out, and if there isn't, you have shown respect.

### Chunk 7: "I need to talk to my partner" or "my accountant"

This may be true, a polite no, or both, and the helpful response is to work with it. Offer to include them ("Would it help if I joined a short call with your partner?"), offer a clear summary they can share, and agree on a follow-up date. Do not try to bypass the other person, because the decision may be shared.

### Chunk 8: When the objection is a real complaint

Sometimes the objection points to a genuine limitation or problem with the product or company. Do not defend it. Thank the person, be honest, and pass it to the team so it's heard, since customer feedback helps the company improve.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. How does fixed pricing change how you respond to "Can you do better on price?"
> 2. Name two things you may not do when responding to a price objection.
> 3. A customer says, "I need to talk to my accountant." What could you offer?

> **Takeaway**
> Responding to the real concern, being straight about price and limits, and passing real complaints on keeps each objection conversation honest.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before your next conversation, write your honest answer to "Can you do better on price?" in one sentence, and say it aloud.

## Lesson 6.4: Respecting a No

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can recognize a clear no, leave gracefully, follow up only as permitted, and learn from objections and refusals.

### Chunk 1: A no is an answer

Not every conversation ends in a yes, and it shouldn't. A person who says no after hearing honest information has made a decision, and that decision deserves respect.

A real no takes several forms: a direct "We're not interested," a repeated no after you've explored and responded, or silence after follow-ups you sent as agreed. A request to stop contacting them is also a real no.

### Chunk 2: How to leave well

> "I appreciate your time, and thank you for being straight with me. If things change, I'd be glad to help. Would it be okay if I check back in a few months, or would you rather I didn't?"

Leaving well means thanking them genuinely, without guilt, argument, or sighing. It also means asking permission to follow up once and accepting the answer, and not making a last-minute offer or inventing urgency.

A person who is treated well when they say no is more likely to say yes later, or to recommend you.

### Chunk 3: Honor requests to stop

If someone asks you to stop contacting them, stop, and record it in the approved system so that no one else on the team contacts them. This is basic respect, and rules on contact exist. Counsel and management will set the written policy.

### Chunk 4: Mixed signals: a soft no

A "soft no" is vague: "maybe later," "send me information," or no reply. Ask once, kindly:

> "It sounds like this may not be the right time. Is that fair to say?"

If they say yes, thank them and leave, and if they say it's just busy, agree on a clear date.

### Chunk 5: Learn from every objection

Keep a simple log of the objections you hear, in plain words. Each entry notes what was said, what it turned out to be (a question, a worry, or a no), what helped, and what the company could do to address it, such as clearer information or a product limit.

Share patterns with your leader. If many people ask the same question, the company can answer it earlier, improve materials, or fix the product, and a rep who hears the same objection ten times is holding useful information.

### Chunk 6: Your own reaction to a no

A no is not a verdict on you. After a hard one, take a minute to walk, breathe, or talk to a teammate, and write down one thing you learned. Then reset before the next contact. If you find yourself dreading or avoiding contact, talk to your leader.

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Objection** | A concern, question, or hesitation a person raises about buying |
| **Hidden objection** | The real concern beneath what a person first says |
| **LAER** | A training model made up of Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, and Respond |
| **Check (step)** | The step of asking whether an answer addressed the concern |
| **Price objection** | A concern about cost or value |
| **Money objection** | A concern about affordability or timing |
| **Source objection** | A concern about the company or the seller |
| **Stall** | A delay such as "let me think about it," which may hide a worry or a no |
| **Soft no** | A polite, vague way of declining |
| **Opt-out / stop request** | A person's request not to be contacted |
| **Objection log** | A short record of objections, what they turned out to be, and what helped |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two signs of a real no.
> 2. What do you do when someone asks you to stop contacting them?
> 3. You've heard the same objection from five customers this month. What should you do with that information, and why?

> **Takeaway**
> A customer who says no after hearing honest information has made a decision, and the professional response is to leave graciously, honor any request to stop contact, and use what the conversation revealed to improve the next one.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After each conversation, write the objection you heard in one line, along with what turned out to matter.
