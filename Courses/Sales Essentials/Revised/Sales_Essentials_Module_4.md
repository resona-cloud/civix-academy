## Lesson 4.1: Why Questions Beat Pitches

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain what discovery is for, describe what you are trying to learn, and say why investigating before presenting makes a conversation better for both people.

### Chunk 1: What discovery is

**Discovery** is the part of the conversation where you learn whether, and how, what you offer fits this person's situation. It happens after the opening and before you present anything.

It serves two purposes. For the customer, it's a chance to describe their situation and realize what matters to them, often for the first time out loud. For you, it's how you learn enough to give honest, relevant help, or to learn that you can't.

### Chunk 2: The problem with pitching first

Compare two moments with the same owner, a fictional hair-salon owner named Lena.

> **Pitch first:**
> "Our platform handles scheduling, reminders, reporting, and payments in one place."
> *Lena's silent reaction:* "I already use a paper book and it works fine. What does this have to do with me?"
>
> **Question first:**
> "What's the most frustrating part of running the schedule right now?"
> *Lena's reaction:* "Honestly? No-shows. I lose about a chair a day when someone doesn't come."

In the first version Lena has to decide, alone, whether any of the features matter. In the second she names the problem herself, and everything you say later has something to attach to.

### Chunk 3: Sellers who investigate first

An open textbook on selling (*Selling for Success*, Nova Scotia Community College) teaches a four-part flow for a call: opening, investigating, demonstrating capability, and obtaining commitment. It says the investigation has to come before the presentation, "or the pitch will miss." The textbook builds that flow on the SPIN research described in the next lesson, so it's a well-organized teaching model and not independent proof. A search for independent studies of questioning in small-sale selling turned up no source reliable enough to cite, so this course teaches discovery on the strength of practice and common sense: it's hard to help someone you haven't listened to.

### Chunk 4: What you are trying to learn

For a small business owner, discovery usually covers six things, though you won't need all of them in one conversation.

| You want to learn | Example question |
|---|---|
| **Situation:** how things work today | "Walk me through how a typical week goes." |
| **Problem:** what is not working | "What's the most frustrating part of that?" |
| **Impact:** what it costs them (time, money, stress, customers) | "What does that cost you when it happens?" |
| **Goal:** what better would look like | "If that were fixed, what would be different?" |
| **Constraints:** what limits their choices | "Is there anything that would make a change hard right now?" |
| **Decision:** who decides, and how | "Who else would weigh in on something like this?" |

### Chunk 5: Honest discovery

Discovery isn't a tool for finding a lever to pull, and two rules keep it honest. Ask because you want to know and not to steer, since if you've already decided what the answer should be, you're pitching with question marks. And be ready for answers that point away from your product: "My real problem is hiring" is useful and true, and it may mean you aren't the right help, which is worth saying plainly.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What are the two purposes of discovery?
> 2. In the Lena example, why does the question-first version work better?
> 3. A prospect describes how their week runs but hasn't said what frustrates them. Which of the six things are you still missing, and what could you ask?

> **Takeaway**
> Learning the person's situation, problem, and goal before presenting gives everything you say later something to attach to, which is why the questions have to be asked because you want to know.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before presenting anything, ask yourself whether you could explain this person's biggest frustration in their own words, and if you can't, ask more.

## Lesson 4.2: A Question Toolkit

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can use open, closed, and probing questions on purpose, apply the four SPIN question types with their limits, and avoid leading, loaded, or interrogating questions.

### Chunk 1: Three kinds of question

| Type | What it is | Good for | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Cannot be answered with yes or no; invites explanation | Getting the story | "How do you handle appointment changes now?" |
| **Closed** | Short answer: yes, no, a number | Confirming facts; narrowing | "Do you have more than one location?" |
| **Probing** | Follows up on something they just said | Going deeper; clarifying | "You said Mondays are chaotic. What happens?" |

A useful rule of thumb is to start open, probe to understand, and then close to confirm. Closed questions shouldn't be used to steer ("You'd like to save time, wouldn't you?"), because that isn't discovery.

### Chunk 2: The SPIN framework

Neil Rackham and colleagues developed a well-known framework for questions from observations of sales calls. It sorts questions into four kinds, and the open textbook gives examples.

| Question type | Purpose | Example (fictional salon) |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Learn basic facts about their current setup | "How many stylists work here, and how do they book clients?" |
| **Problem** | Help them name a difficulty or dissatisfaction | "What's hardest about handling the booking book?" |
| **Implication** | Explore what the problem leads to | "When someone no-shows, what happens to the rest of that day?" |
| **Need-payoff** | Let them say what a solution would be worth | "If you could fill that gap, what would that mean for you?" |

### Chunk 3: Using SPIN well, and its limits

SPIN gets some things right. It moves from facts to difficulties, then to consequences, and then to benefits, and it lets the customer state the value. It also encourages you to ask more and tell less.

There are limits to keep in mind, drawn from the open textbook's cautions and from this course's own. Situation questions work best sparingly, because too many bore people, and doing the research first means you don't ask what you could have looked up. Don't assume the person sees the problem just because you do; ask and don't tell, and investigate before presenting benefits, or the pitch will miss.

Implication questions must be honest. They exist to help a person see real consequences and not to create fear or inflate a problem, and if you're exaggerating to build pressure, stop, because that is the pressure tactic covered in Module 1.

The research behind SPIN has limits as well. The best-known source is the framework's own training company, which describes the original research as concerning major, high-value sales and not small ones, and it gives few statistical details, so its claims can't be checked. SPIN is therefore best treated as a useful way to organize questions and not as proven science or a script. A conversation with a small business owner often takes two or three minutes of questions and not a full SPIN sequence.

### Chunk 4: Questions that go wrong

| Problem | Example | Better |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** (suggests the answer) | "Wouldn't you agree your schedule is a mess?" | "How would you describe your schedule right now?" |
| **Loaded** (assumes something) | "When did you start losing money on no-shows?" | "How much of an issue are no-shows?" |
| **Stacked** (several at once) | "How many stylists, and what software, and what's your budget?" | One at a time |
| **Interrogating** (rapid-fire) | A string of questions with no response in between | Pause, acknowledge, then ask |
| **Closed when you needed open** | "Do you have scheduling problems?" | "What scheduling challenges come up?" |
| **Too personal or not your business** | "What do you earn?" | Ask about the business need, not private finances |

### Chunk 5: Questions that open people up

Some prompts work well because they're natural. "Tell me more about that" invites detail, and "What does a good week look like? And a bad one?" gets at the range of their experience. "What have you tried before?" followed by "What happened?" shows what they already know, and "What's the one thing you'd change if you could?" surfaces priorities. Two others are "What would make this worth looking at, and what would make it not worth your time?" and "Is there something I should be asking that I haven't?"

### Chunk 6: A short fictional exchange

> **Jordan:** "Lena, thanks for the time. Before I say anything about us, can I ask a few questions about how you run the schedule?"
> **Lena:** "Sure."
> **Jordan:** "How do clients book with you today?"
> **Lena:** "Phone, text, walk-ins. I write it in a book."
> **Jordan:** "What's the most frustrating part of that?"
> **Lena:** "No-shows. I lose a chair a day, sometimes."
> **Jordan:** "That sounds painful. What happens to the rest of that day when it happens?"
> **Lena:** "I end up scrambling, and I'm there late."
> **Jordan:** "If you didn't have to scramble, what would that give you?"
> **Lena:** "My evenings back."

Jordan never mentioned a product, and Lena still named the problem, its effect, and what she wants.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is the difference between an open and a probing question?
> 2. Name the four SPIN question types.
> 3. Rewrite this to remove the leading: "Wouldn't you agree your schedule is a mess?"

> **Takeaway**
> Starting open, probing, and then confirming gives you the person's story, and SPIN can organize the questions as long as you ask honestly and never manufacture pressure.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Prepare three open questions for the next conversation, and write them where you can see them.

## Lesson 4.3: Listening for the Real Need

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can use paraphrasing, summarizing, and clarifying to confirm you understood, and tell the difference between what someone says and what they need.

### Chunk 1: What people say and what they need

The first answer is often not the whole answer.

| What they say | What may be underneath |
|---|---|
| "It's too expensive." | "I'm not sure it will work for me." |
| "We're fine." | "I haven't thought about it." or "Not now." |
| "I need to talk to my partner." | A real second decision-maker, or a polite no |
| "We tried something like this." | "It went badly, and I'm wary." |
| "Can you send me information?" | Interest, or a polite way to end the call |

You find out by asking kindly ("What happened last time?") and not by assuming.

### Chunk 2: Active listening skills

A counselor-written overview of active listening lists these core skills, which transfer well to sales.

| Skill | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Paraphrasing** | Restate what you heard in your own words | "So no-shows are your biggest drain." |
| **Summarizing** | Pull together the key points | "Let me check I've got it: bookings come by phone and text, no-shows cost you a chair a day, and you'd like your evenings back." |
| **Reflecting** | Repeat the essence, including the feeling, without parroting | "It sounds like that's stressful." |
| **Clarifying** | Ask about anything vague | "When you say 'a mess,' what does that look like?" |
| **Open questions and encouragers** | Keep them talking | "Tell me more." "And then?" |
| **Attending to tone and body language** | Notice how it is said, as a clue | "I noticed you slowed down there. Is that a sore spot?" |

The overview comes from a counselor's website and draws on counseling and communication research, most of which concerns counseling and healthcare and not sales. The skills are widely taught, so it's reasonable to use them here.

### Chunk 3: Say it back

Saying back what you heard does three things: it shows you're listening, it corrects misunderstandings early, and it often prompts the person to add something important. A simple form makes it a habit:

> "Let me make sure I have this right. [Their situation.] [Their problem.] [What they want.] Did I get that right?"

If they say "not exactly," that's useful, and you can ask, "What did I miss?"

### Chunk 4: Habits that get in the way

Several habits get in the way of listening. Planning your answer while they talk means you miss the second half, and finishing their sentences feels presumptuous. Jumping to a solution the moment you hear a problem cuts the discovery short, and correcting someone when what they said is not quite right can derail them, so it's usually better to note it and move on unless it matters. Filling silences is another, since a pause often leads to the most honest part of the answer, and checking a phone or the room tells the person they don't have your attention.

### Chunk 5: Take notes, and say you are

Write down what matters: their words, the problem, what they want, and any worries. Saying "Mind if I jot that down?" shows respect, and the notes make your follow-up specific. Record only business-relevant, non-sensitive information.

### Chunk 6: Listen for what limits their choices

Besides the problem, listen for what limits their choices. Time shows up in remarks like "We can't take anything on until after the holidays," and money in general terms in "Cash is tight right now." Price is fixed, so you don't negotiate it, but you can be honest about what the plan costs and when it starts. People matter too ("My staff hates new tools"), as does history ("We got burned by a contract"), and so does the decision process ("I run everything past my accountant").

These limits tell you how to present honestly and what to avoid promising.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Give an example of how a first answer may not be the whole answer.
> 2. What are paraphrasing and summarizing?
> 3. A prospect says, "We tried something like this before." What could you ask, and why not assume what they mean?

> **Takeaway**
> The need behind the words only comes out when you listen, say it back, and ask kindly about anything unclear.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After the person finishes describing their situation, pause, summarize it in two sentences, and ask, "Did I get that right?"

## Lesson 4.4: When to Stop Asking

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can tell when you have learned enough, check whether the product truly fits, move from discovery to presenting with permission, and end politely when it does not fit.

### Chunk 1: When you have enough

You don't need to ask forever. You can stop asking when you can honestly say that you understand their situation and the problem they care about, and that you know what they would like to change. You also need to know whether this could fit and what to avoid promising, and who decides and when. Over-asking turns a conversation into an interrogation and uses up their time.

### Chunk 2: A quick fit check

Use what you know about who the product helps and who it doesn't, and ask yourself the following questions.

| Check | Question to yourself |
|---|---|
| **A real problem?** | Did they describe something that matters to them? |
| **A fit?** | Does what we offer actually address it? |
| **The right person?** | Are they the one who decides, or will someone else weigh in? |
| **Timing?** | Is this a now thing, a later thing, or never? |
| **Able and willing to commit?** | The plan is a fixed-price subscription. Does that work for them in general terms, without you probing their finances? |
| **Reasonable expectations?** | Do they expect something we cannot honestly deliver? |

A venture investor's article argues that spotting poor-fit customers early saves time and builds trust. It's opinion with no data and it's written about larger business sales, so only the idea carries over.

### Chunk 3: Moving from discovery to presenting

Before you present, summarize and ask permission:

> "Based on what you've told me, it sounds like [situation, problem, goal]. Would it be useful if I showed you how some owners handle that, and you tell me whether it fits?"

This keeps the person in control and signals that what comes next is tied to their words.

### Chunk 4: When it is not a fit

Saying "this is probably not right for you" is a strength, because it earns trust and saves everyone time. It works best when done kindly and clearly:

> "Based on what you've told me, I don't think this is the right fit. Your biggest challenge is hiring, and that isn't something we help with. I'd rather tell you now than waste your time. If something changes, I'm happy to talk again."

Where you can, point to a better resource, such as a type of provider or a general source of help, without recommending a specific company unless Resona has an approved referral. Do not leave a poor-fit customer with a sale they'll regret, since it leads to refunds, complaints, and a bad name.

### Chunk 5: When it is a fit but not now

When it's a fit but the timing is wrong, agree on a specific next step with a date, and note what the person said they need to see before deciding. Don't push, and follow up as agreed.

### Chunk 6: Record what you learned

Write a short note right after the conversation covering their situation, the problem in their words, what they want, their constraints, who decides, and the next step. Keep it business-relevant and non-sensitive, and store it in the approved system.

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Discovery** | The part of a conversation in which you learn whether and how your product fits the person's situation |
| **Open question** | A question that invites explanation |
| **Closed question** | A question with a short answer such as yes, no, or a number |
| **Probing question** | A follow-up that digs into something the person just said |
| **SPIN** | A question framework made up of Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-payoff questions |
| **Leading question** | A question that suggests the answer you want |
| **Loaded question** | A question that assumes something not yet established |
| **Paraphrasing** | A listening skill that restates what you heard in your own words |
| **Summarizing** | A listening skill that pulls together the key points to check understanding |
| **Reflecting** | A listening skill that repeats the essence of what was said, including the feeling |
| **Underlying need** | The real concern beneath the first thing a person says |
| **Fit check** | A quick review of whether the product suits the person |
| **Poor fit** | A situation in which the product is unlikely to serve the person well |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two signs you have asked enough.
> 2. What do you say before moving from discovery to presenting?
> 3. A prospect's biggest problem is hiring, which your product doesn't address. What do you say, and why is saying it a strength?

> **Takeaway**
> Asking until you understand, checking the fit honestly, and being willing to say "not a fit" is what lets you ask permission to present with confidence.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> End each discovery with a two-sentence summary and a question: "Did I get that right? May I show you how this might help?"
