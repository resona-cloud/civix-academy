## Lesson 5.1: Features, Benefits, and the Need They Answer

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can turn a product feature into a benefit that answers something the customer said, choose a few points instead of listing everything, and avoid presenting before you have investigated.

### Chunk 1: Presenting comes after discovery

Discovery tells you what the person's situation, problem, and goal are. Presenting is where you connect what you offer to what they told you, and it isn't a tour of everything the product does.

The open textbook *Selling for Success* (NSCC) makes the same point: investigate first, then show how your product addresses the stated need, because benefits work better than generic advantages when they're tailored to the buyer. It also notes that two buyers may buy the same product for different reasons.

### Chunk 2: Features and benefits

| Term | Meaning | Example (fictional scheduling tool for a salon) |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | What the product is or does | "It sends text reminders to clients." |
| **Benefit** | What that does for this person | "So fewer clients forget, and you don't lose a chair a day." |

A widely taught version of this idea is called **features, advantages, and benefits (FAB)**, which covers what the product is, what it does, and what that means for the customer. The textbook used for this course doesn't use the FAB label, so the course uses the simpler pair of feature and benefit and adds one test.

> **The "so what?" test.** After every feature you mention, ask yourself, "So what does that mean for them?" If you can't answer in their words, leave the feature out.

### Chunk 3: Connect it to their words

A benefit is strongest when it echoes what the customer said. Lena said that no-shows cost her a chair a day and that she wants her evenings back.

| Weak (generic) | Strong (tied to what she said) |
|---|---|
| "Our platform has automated reminders." | "You mentioned no-shows cost you a chair a day. Reminders go out automatically, so fewer clients forget, which could get you home on time more evenings." |

The wording in the strong version is careful: it says "could" and not "will." You can describe what the product is designed to do and what other customers have experienced, but you can't promise Lena's results, a limit the claims lesson below explains.

### Chunk 4: Choose a few points

A pitch that covers twenty features sounds like noise, so it helps to work in three steps. List what they told you matters, which is usually two or three things, then match one feature to each and say the benefit in their terms. After that, stop and ask whether it addresses what they care about.

If a feature doesn't connect to anything they said, don't mention it unless they ask. You can always say, "There's more, and I'm happy to show you anything you're curious about."

### Chunk 5: Speak to different people differently

An owner who cares about time wants to hear about hours saved, and one who cares about reputation wants to hear about customer experience. One who is wary wants to hear what happens if it doesn't work. It's the same product with different benefits, chosen from what each person told you.

### Chunk 6: Be honest about limits

Presenting value includes saying what the product does not do:

> "This helps with reminders and bookings. It doesn't handle payroll, so you'd keep using your current tool for that."

That honesty builds credibility and prevents refunds and complaints.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is the difference between a feature and a benefit?
> 2. A customer told you they dread month-end bookkeeping. Your product has a dozen features. How do you decide which to mention?
> 3. Why say "could" and not "will" when describing what a customer might gain?

> **Takeaway**
> Matching a few features to what the customer said and stating the benefit in their words keeps a presentation relevant, and being honest about limits keeps it credible.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before presenting, write the two or three things the customer said matter most, match one feature to each, and present only those.

## Lesson 5.2: Proof: Examples, Evidence, and Honest Comparisons

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can offer proof that is real and relevant, quantify value using the customer's own numbers, and compare to alternatives without misleading.

### Chunk 1: Why proof matters

Saying a product helps is easy, and a small business owner has heard it before. Proof is what makes it believable, and good proof has five qualities: it's real, meaning it actually happened, and relevant, meaning it resembles this customer's situation. It's also specific, with details and not adjectives, current, and not out of date, and checkable, so they could verify it if they wanted.

### Chunk 2: Types of proof

| Type | What it is | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Demonstration** | Showing the product doing the thing, ideally with the customer's own situation | Staged examples that hide limits |
| **Customer example** | What another business did and what happened | Must be true, relevant, with permission, and not the unusual best case |
| **Documented results** | Numbers the company can substantiate | Must be accurate, current, and not overstated |
| **Trial or sample** | Letting them try it | Terms must be clear |
| **Third-party review or recognition** | What others have said or awarded | Must be genuine and properly disclosed |

The open textbook's guidance is that letting a prospect experience the product is one of the strongest forms of proof, since it educates, involves them, and shows performance.

### Chunk 3: Use the customer's own numbers

The same textbook suggests quantifying value, such as return on investment (ROI), using a worked example of a purchase with a cost and annual savings. That example is written for general products and uses invented figures, so it shouldn't be reused.

The safest way to quantify value in this work is to use the customer's numbers and not yours. Ask, then calculate together:

> "You said you lose about one chair a day to no-shows. If a chair is worth about [your number] a day, that's roughly [their number] a week. Does that sound right?"

Then make an if/then statement and leave the conclusion to them:

> "If reminders cut that in half, what would that be worth to you?"

Do not supply a figure for what they will save. You don't know their business, and a number you invent is a claim you would have to prove.

### Chunk 4: Honest comparisons

Customers will compare you to other options, including doing nothing. A fair comparison names the alternatives accurately, including a spreadsheet, a paper book, or hiring help, and it says what each does well and not only what it does badly. It also avoids attacking competitors, since a vendor article on pressure tactics and the FTC's rules both warn that false statements about a competitor can create legal risk, and even true attacks sound petty. Finally, it compares like with like and doesn't claim "best" or "only" unless you can prove it.

> "Plenty of owners start with a paper book, and if you have two or three clients a day, that works. When it gets to fifteen, it gets hard to keep track. That's where this helps."

### Chunk 5: If you do not have proof

Say so. "I don't have an example from a shop like yours, but I can show you how it works and you can judge" is a fair answer, and so is offering a trial if the company has one. Do not stretch an example to fit.

### Chunk 6: Permission and privacy

Before mentioning a customer's name or results to another customer, make sure you have their permission, and use only what they agreed to share. Do not share what a customer told you in confidence. If you're not sure you have permission, describe the example without identifying details, or leave it out.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three qualities of good proof.
> 2. Why should you use the customer's own numbers when quantifying value?
> 3. A prospect asks for an example from a business like theirs, and you don't have one. What do you say?

> **Takeaway**
> Real, relevant, specific proof carries more weight than adjectives, and using the customer's own numbers and comparing fairly keeps it honest.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before using a customer story, check three things: whether it's true, whether it resembles this person, and whether you have permission to share it.

## Lesson 5.3: Claims, Testimonials, and Disclosure

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain the "reasonable basis" rule for claims, why a "results may vary" note does not fix an unrepresentative testimonial, and what you may and may not do about reviews.

> **Note:** This lesson summarizes public guidance from the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and law-firm summaries of a 2024 rule. It is not legal advice. State laws can add more. The lesson ends with an open item for Resona and counsel.

### Chunk 1: The basic rule

In the United States, a business that makes **objective claims** about its product, such as "reduces no-shows" or "saves five hours a week," is expected to have a reasonable basis for the claim before making it. The FTC's policy statement on advertising substantiation says that failing to have and rely on a reasonable basis for objective claims is an unfair and deceptive practice. The rule reaches claims made in conversation by salespeople as well as in ads, because what matters is whether a claim misleads.

The policy statement makes three points that matter here. If you make an express claim such as "tests prove," "studies show," or "most customers," you need at least that level of support. A statement can also imply more than it says, so you need support for what the customer will reasonably take from it. And when no level of support is stated, customers still expect a reasonable basis, with what counts depending on the type of claim, the product, the consequences of being wrong, and what experts in the field would consider reasonable.

The statement dates from 1983, and the copy reviewed for this course was a 2009 printout, so check that it is still current before relying on it.

### Chunk 2: What this means for you

Every statement about results needs a source.

| You say | You need |
|---|---|
| "It reduces no-shows." | A basis, such as documented results or testing the company can show |
| "Most customers save time." | Proof that most do, not that some did |
| "You'll see results in a week." | Proof that this is typical, or do not say it |
| "Customers love it." | A real basis, such as review data, and not one or two stories |
| "The only tool that does X." | Proof that nothing else does, which is hard, so avoid it |

If you don't have the basis, say what you do know: what the product does, what the plan includes, and how it works.

### Chunk 3: Testimonials and typical results

A customer story can mislead if it describes an unusual result. The FTC's Endorsement Guides FAQ (2015) makes three points. People usually read a specific testimonial as typical, and "individual results may vary" doesn't change that reading. The business must either have proof that the result is typical or clearly disclose what results are generally expected. And older testimonials must still be accurate, because if the product has changed, the story may no longer be fair.

The Endorsement Guides were revised in 2023. The FTC page reviewed for this course confirms that the Guides aren't regulations, but that practices inconsistent with them can lead to enforcement under the FTC Act.

For you, that means never presenting a customer's best result as what a new customer should expect. A fair way to say it is, "That was one owner's experience. It's not a promise of what you'll see."

### Chunk 4: Connections must be disclosed

If you recommend or review a product and you have a relationship with the seller that could affect how people weigh your view, such as being paid, being an employee, or receiving free product, that connection must be disclosed clearly. The FTC FAQ states that employees must disclose they work for the company when endorsing its products, and that a note buried in a profile is not enough.

You work with or for Resona, so when you tell someone a product is good, that is a sales conversation and they know it. Do not post reviews or recommendations of Resona products on public sites, forums, or social media as though you were an independent user. If you post about your work, say who you are.

### Chunk 5: Reviews: a 2024 rule

The FTC finalized a rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, effective October 21, 2024. According to a law-firm summary of the rule, it prohibits fake or false reviews and testimonials, including ones that misrepresent whether the reviewer used the product or what happened. It also prohibits paying for reviews with a particular sentiment: offering a discount for a review isn't unlawful in itself, but tying it to a positive (or negative) review is. The remaining prohibitions cover undisclosed reviews by insiders such as officers, managers, and employees, and company-controlled review sites presented as independent. The rule also covers suppressing negative reviews, with narrow exceptions, and presenting selected favorable reviews as representative, and fake social media followers, likes, or views used to misrepresent influence.

The rule carries civil penalties per violation. The summary reviewed gave an amount as of 2024, and it's adjusted yearly, so do not quote a number.

These practical rules apply to our team.

- Never write, post, or ask for a fake review.
- Never offer a discount, credit, or gift in exchange for a positive review.
- You may invite honest feedback from real customers after real use, without telling them what to say.
- Do not ask a customer to remove an honest negative review. Fix the problem and tell your leader.
- Employees and contractors do not review the company's products as though independent.
- Do not buy followers, likes, or views.

### Chunk 6: When you are unsure

Ask before you say or post it. A good question to put to your leader is this:

> "Do we have proof for this statement, and is it okay to say it to customers?"

An open item remains for Resona and counsel: a short written list of approved claims, with the proof behind each one, and a written reviews and testimonials policy covering who may post what and how customer stories are approved and disclosed. Until then, say only what the product plainly does and what the plan includes.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What does "reasonable basis" mean for a claim?
> 2. A customer's story describes an unusually large result, and a teammate wants to add "results may vary" and use it as is. Why isn't that enough?
> 3. Name two things our team never does about reviews.

> **Takeaway**
> Every claim needs a reasonable basis, and testimonials have to be fair, connections disclosed, and reviews real, because each of these protects the customer from being misled.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you say "customers usually" or "most people," ask yourself whether the company can show that, and if it can't, describe a real example as one example or leave it out.

## Lesson 5.4: Making It Easy to Understand

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain things in plain language, show instead of tell, check understanding as you go, and keep a presentation short and focused on the customer.

### Chunk 1: Clear beats clever

An owner who doesn't understand something won't buy it, and will rarely say so. Short sentences and everyday words help, so say "send reminders" and not "leverage automated notification workflows." Keep to one idea at a time, use their words from discovery, and explain any term you must use once and simply. A concrete example from their business works better than an abstract one.

### Chunk 2: Show instead of tell

The open textbook says that letting prospects experience the product is among the most powerful tools, since it educates them and proves performance. In practice, that means showing the one thing that matters most to them first and not giving a tour from the top menu. It also means using their situation ("Let's say Lena books a haircut for Tuesday at 2. Here's what she sees"), letting them touch it or tell you what to try, and showing the limits too, so there are no surprises later.

A full demonstration structure for Resona products is taught in the Resona Product Demo course. This lesson covers the basics that apply to any conversation.

### Chunk 3: Check understanding as you go

Short questions keep you honest about whether you've been clear: "Does that make sense so far?", "How would that work in your shop?", and "What questions come to mind?" The most revealing is "In your own words, what would that change for you?" because if they can't say it back, you haven't made it clear yet.

### Chunk 4: Keep it short

The textbook offers a rule attributed to a well-known author for slide presentations: no more than 10 slides, about 20 minutes, and a large font. It's best taken as a reminder to be brief. For a small business owner, a presentation is often a few minutes, and a slide deck may not be needed at all.

Customers want you and not a recitation, so don't rely on slides or a script. Face the customer instead of hiding behind a screen, and give them a short, useful leave-behind and not a stack of pages. Clutter, gimmicks, and distracting transitions are better avoided.

### Chunk 5: Video and phone presentations

A video call deserves the same care as an in-person meeting: be prepared, minimize distractions, and close with next steps. On the phone, use simple words and check in more often, since you can't see their face. In both, avoid multitasking, because they can tell.

### Chunk 6: After you present

Don't rush to close. Ask instead:

> "How does this compare with what you were hoping for?"

Their answer tells you whether to move to a next step, address a concern, or stop.

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Feature** | A description of what a product is or does |
| **Benefit** | What a feature does for this particular customer |
| **"So what?" test** | A check that asks what a feature means for the customer, and drops it if you can't say |
| **Proof** | Real, relevant, specific, current, and checkable support for a claim |
| **ROI** | Return on investment, meaning what a customer gains compared with what they spend |
| **Objective claim** | A statement of fact about a product or result that can be proved or disproved |
| **Reasonable basis** | The support a business must have before making an objective claim |
| **Substantiation** | The evidence that backs a claim |
| **Testimonial** | A customer's statement about their experience with a product |
| **Typical results** | What most customers can generally expect |
| **Material connection** | A relationship with a seller that could affect how people weigh a recommendation |
| **Disclosure** | A clear statement of a connection or a limitation |
| **Demonstration** | A showing of the product doing something in a customer's situation |
| **Plain language** | Clear, short, everyday words |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Give two ways to make an explanation easier to understand.
> 2. What question reveals whether the person understood?
> 3. A prospect nods through your whole explanation but asks nothing. What could you ask to find out whether they understood?

> **Takeaway**
> Plain words, one clear demonstration of what matters most, and regular checks for understanding keep a presentation short and keep it about the customer.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After presenting, ask "In your own words, what would this change for you?" and listen carefully to the answer.

**Q1.** Which best describes a benefit?
- A. What the product is made of
- B. A technical specification of the product
- C. A list of everything the product can do
- **D. What a feature does for this particular customer, in their terms** (correct)

**Q2.** After you mention a feature, what should you ask yourself?
- A. "Can I say more about it?"
- B. "Is this longer than my competitor's description?"
- **C. "So what does that mean for them?"** (correct)
- D. "Have I covered the whole menu?"

**Q3.** The customer said no-shows cost them a chair a day. Which statement is the best way to connect a feature to that?
- **A. "Reminders go out automatically, so fewer clients forget, which could help with the no-shows you mentioned."** (correct)
- B. "Our platform has many features."
- C. "You'll never have a no-show again."
- D. "Most customers love reminders."

**Q4.** What is the safest way to quantify value for a customer?
- A. Give them a savings figure that sounds impressive
- B. Quote an average from a different industry
- C. Avoid mentioning numbers at all
- **D. Use the customer's own numbers, ask them to confirm, and let them reach the conclusion** (correct)

**Q5.** What does the FTC policy statement say about objective claims?
- A. Claims need no support if they are made in conversation
- B. Claims are acceptable whenever the customer asks for them
- **C. A business should have a reasonable basis for an objective claim before making it** (correct)
- D. Only written claims need support

**Q6.** A customer's testimonial describes an unusual result. What does the guidance say about a "results may vary" note?
- A. It fixes the problem
- **B. It generally does not change how people read the testimonial; the business needs proof the result is typical or a clear statement of what is generally expected** (correct)
- C. It is required on every testimonial
- D. It is only needed when the testimonial is in writing

**Q7.** A teammate suggests offering a discount to customers who leave a five-star review. What is the right response?
- A. Agree, since it is a standard tactic
- B. Offer it only to loyal customers
- C. Offer it, but keep it off the record
- **D. Decline; tying a reward to a positive review is a problem, and inviting honest feedback is fine** (correct)

**Q8.** You are asked "Is this the only tool that does this?" and you do not know. What do you say?
- A. "Yes, it's the only one."
- **B. "I don't know every other option. Here's what this does, and you can compare."** (correct)
- C. "Definitely, nobody else does."
- D. "Don't worry about other tools."
