## Lesson 3.1: The First Thirty Seconds

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can open a conversation by saying who you are, why you are there, and asking permission to continue, and you can name common openers that backfire.

### Chunk 1: What an opening has to do

A small business owner is usually in the middle of something when you appear, and in the first moments they are silently asking three questions: who you are, what you want, and how long this will take. A good opening answers all three quickly, honestly, and politely, and then asks permission to continue. It doesn't try to sell anything yet.

### Chunk 2: A simple structure

| Part | What you say | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| **Name** | Your name and company | "Hi, I'm Jordan with Resona." |
| **Reason** | Why you are reaching out, in one sentence | "I'm talking with local shop owners about how they handle their appointment scheduling." |
| **Why them (optional)** | One fact from your preparation | "I saw you opened a second chair this year." |
| **Permission and time** | Ask whether now works and say how long | "Do you have two minutes, or is there a better time?" |

That's the whole structure, and once you've said it, the owner decides what happens next.

### Chunk 3: Details that matter

A Canadian open textbook on selling (*Selling for Success*, NSCC, 2023) lists habits that apply to every kind of opening. Getting the name right matters, because spelling and pronunciation errors signal that you didn't care enough to check. The textbook also quotes the advice "Don't be interesting; be interested," since questions and follow-ups show real interest, and it recommends having a one-minute description of what you do ready, so you can answer "what is this about?" without rambling.

For phone openings, the same textbook suggests stating your name and purpose within about 20 seconds, using a prepared script. It also advises asking "Is this a good time?" and not a question that invites an easy no, such as "Did I catch you at a bad time?" It recommends skipping "How are you today?" as a cold opener, because it sounds like a script, and something like "Thanks for taking my call" works better. Long explanations are best avoided, since busy decision-makers see cold calls as interruptions.

### Chunk 4: Openers that backfire

| Opener | Why it backfires |
|---|---|
| "I'm not trying to sell you anything" (when you are) | Misleading, and people hear it as a sales line |
| "How are you today?" read in a flat voice | Signals a script |
| A long description of the company | Makes the person wait to learn why you are there |
| "Is the owner in?" with no name or reason | Gatekeepers hear it as a pitch and the owner hears it as an interruption |
| An unrelated compliment you do not mean | Fake rapport |
| Launching into features | Skips the person completely |

### Chunk 5: If the answer is "not now"

"Not now" is a complete, fair answer, and a good reply sounds like this:

> "No problem at all. When would be better, or would you rather I send a short note first?"

Then do exactly what you offered. A person who is treated well when they say "not now" is much more likely to say yes later.

### Chunk 6: Practice the opener

Write yours in two sentences, say it aloud, and time it. If it takes more than about twenty seconds, cut it.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name the three things an owner wants to know in the first moments.
> 2. You're calling a shop owner you've never spoken to. Which of these opens better, "Is this a good time?" or "Did I catch you at a bad time?", and why?
> 3. What should you do when someone says "not now"?

> **Takeaway**
> A good opening says who you are and why you're there and then asks permission, which leaves the decision about what happens next with the person you're talking to.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Write your opener, read it aloud, and cut every word the person doesn't need in the first twenty seconds.

## Lesson 3.2: Rapport Without Fakery

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe how rapport is built from real interest, listening, and respect, and say why technique-driven tricks tend to backfire.

### Chunk 1: What rapport is

**Rapport** is the sense that the other person is comfortable with you and that you're on the same side. It isn't charm or a trick; it grows when a person feels heard, respected, and not rushed.

High self-orientation lowers trust, and rapport works in the same way: the less you appear to be working an angle, the more people relax.

### Chunk 2: What builds it

| Habit | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Genuine interest** | Asking about their business and caring about the answer |
| **Listening fully** | Not interrupting; not planning your reply while they talk |
| **Using their name** | Spelled and pronounced correctly |
| **Matching their pace** | Brief with a busy owner, more relaxed with someone who wants to talk |
| **Respecting their time** | Saying how long you need, and keeping to it |
| **Finding real common ground** | Noticing something you actually share, such as a neighborhood or a shared challenge |
| **Warmth and humor, naturally** | A smile, a light comment, but never at their expense |
| **Following through** | Doing what you said you would do |

### Chunk 3: Listening that shows

People can tell whether you're listening, and a few simple habits make it visible. Pausing for a second before you answer tells the person that you thought about what they said. Saying back the key point ("So the biggest headache is the Monday schedule changes") confirms you heard it, and a follow-up question proves you caught the detail. Putting the phone away and facing the person helps, as does taking a brief note and saying so: "Mind if I jot that down?"

### Chunk 4: A caution about mirroring and other techniques

You'll meet advice to copy the other person's posture, gestures, and speech to create rapport, and some of it is harmless. A former FBI behavioral analyst writing in *Psychology Today* describes people naturally mirroring each other when they're comfortable. That article is a blog post based on anecdotes, it cites no studies, and a number of its claims have no support, so it's best treated as a description of what comfort sometimes looks like and not as a method.

There are three reasons not to rely on technique. Forced mimicry looks odd, and people notice. It also shifts your attention to yourself instead of the customer, which raises self-orientation. And gestures are ambiguous, since crossed arms can mean discomfort, a cold room, or simple habit. If you want one rule, it's to be natural, be interested, and let your body language follow your attention.

### Chunk 5: Small talk, in measure

Small talk works when it's short, relevant, and optional. A comment about something visible and positive ("Great storefront") is fine, and you should move on if the person is busy. Follow their lead: if they want to chat, chat for a minute, and if they're watching the door, get to the point. Avoid sensitive subjects such as politics, religion, and personal matters, and do not flatter falsely. If you can't say it honestly, don't say it.

### Chunk 6: Respect is the foundation

Respect shows in small things: arriving when you said, not interrupting their work with customers, not touching their things, and accepting a no gracefully. Rapport that rests on respect survives disagreement, whereas rapport that rests on technique does not.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three habits that build rapport.
> 2. A colleague says they were trained to copy a customer's posture to build rapport. What problems could that cause?
> 3. What is one way to show you are listening?

> **Takeaway**
> Real interest, full listening, and respect build rapport that lasts, whereas tricks tend to backfire because they shift your attention from the customer to yourself.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> In your next conversation, count how many times you say back something the person said, and aim for at least two.

## Lesson 3.3: Reading Signals

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can recognize common signs of interest, hesitation, and "not now," and respond by checking in instead of guessing.

### Chunk 1: Signals are clues, not answers

People show how they feel through words, tone, pace, and body language. These are clues, and they can be misread, so the best practice is to notice and then check.

The *Psychology Today* article from the previous lesson lists cues such as leaning in, open posture, and nodding as signs of comfort, and crossed arms or objects placed between people as signs of distance. Its evidence is anecdotal, and the author's own examples show many exceptions, so the cues are best used only to prompt a question.

### Chunk 2: A signal guide

| What you notice | It might mean | A good response |
|---|---|---|
| Asks questions about details or how it works | Interest | Answer simply, then ask what matters most to them |
| Leans in, nods, says "go on" | Engaged | Keep going, and invite their view |
| Short answers, looking away, checking the time | Rushed or uninterested | Say: "I can see you're busy. Should I come back, or give you the one-minute version?" |
| Interrupts you | Impatient or has a concern | Stop and ask what they were about to say |
| Silence | Thinking, or lost | Wait a few seconds, then: "What's on your mind?" |
| Long list of "what ifs" | Worry | Ask which worry matters most |
| Polite but vague ("sounds interesting") | Possibly no, softly | Ask: "What would you want to see to know if it's worth a closer look?" |
| Glances at customers, employees, or the door | Their attention is elsewhere | Offer to pause or follow up |
| Warmth and humor | Comfort | Stay natural and do not push |

### Chunk 3: Check in out loud

You don't have to guess. Short check-ins are respectful and effective, and a few examples are "Is this useful so far?", "Am I explaining this clearly?", "What questions come to mind?", and "Would it help to stop here and pick it up another time?"

A check-in gives the person an easy way to steer you. Many people will say "I'm just busy right now," and you'll learn it isn't a no.

### Chunk 4: Hear the real "no"

A polite owner may not say no directly. Several phrases often mean "no, thank you": "Send me some information" with nothing more, "Let me think about it" with no question asked, "Maybe next quarter" with no reason, and not returning calls after a clear interest.

These are best treated as honest signals and not as tricks. Ask once, kindly, and respect the answer: "It sounds like this may not be the right time or fit. Is that fair to say?"

### Chunk 5: Take your own signals seriously

Notice yourself too. If you feel the urge to speed up, talk more, or push, that's a signal to slow down and ask a question.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Why is it risky to rely on body language alone?
> 2. A prospect gives short answers and keeps checking the time. What could you say?
> 3. What are two phrases that often mean a polite no?

> **Takeaway**
> Signals are clues and not answers, so noticing them and then checking in out loud works better than guessing, and a soft no deserves to be treated as a real answer.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> If you're unsure how a person feels, say so kindly: "I can't tell if this is useful. Can you tell me honestly?"

## Lesson 3.4: Different Settings

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can adapt your opening to in-person, phone, video, and written contact, and describe the basic rules about unsolicited calls and texts.

### Chunk 1: In person

Walking into someone's business makes you a guest in their workplace, so start by reading the room. A line of customers or a rush is the wrong time, and the right move is to offer to come back. Never interrupt a customer interaction to talk to the owner. Ask for the right person politely ("Is the owner or manager available for two minutes?") and respect the answer, then introduce yourself briefly with your name, company, and purpose.

If they ask, leave something simple behind, such as a card or short note with your contact details and what to expect. Noticing the setting, including the space, the hours, and how things work, helps you ask better questions later. And be easy to leave: thank them and go when they signal they're done.

### Chunk 2: Phone

The same open textbook recommends being brief and direct on the phone, since rapport is harder without visual cues. State your name and purpose within about 20 seconds, ask whether it's a good time and offer to call back, and avoid long explanations. A few practical points add to that: stand or sit upright, because it affects your voice, speak a bit slower than feels natural, and have your goal and notes in front of you.

### Chunk 3: Video

A marketing agency's guide to video sales calls (the agency sells inbound-marketing training, and the post dates from 2020) gives practical points that are common sense for any video call. Test your setup first, including the camera, microphone, and meeting link, and face your light source, since a window behind you leaves you in shadow. Sit upright and frame yourself so your face is visible, and smile and look at the camera sometimes, because on video a neutral face can look serious. If the person is unsure how the tool works, explain it, use their name, and invite them into the conversation. Address distractions politely and keep to the time you said.

The post claims that close rates are higher with cameras on but cites no source, so that claim isn't used here. Do not require a customer to turn on their camera. Offering is fine, and some people can't or prefer not to.

### Chunk 4: Email, text, and social

According to the open textbook, written first contact is a first impression. Proofread, use the person's name correctly, and personalize every message so it doesn't look like a template. Keep it short, with one clear ask. Persistence is fine and pestering is not, so space out follow-ups and stop when asked. On social networks, personalize connection requests, contribute value before pitching, and remember that anything you post can be seen by customers.

### Chunk 5: Rules about unsolicited calls and texts

Rules about phone calls, text messages, and email to people you haven't spoken with before exist, and they can be strict. This is a legal area, and what follows is a general orientation and not legal advice. The rules vary and change, and the primary sources were not read for this course.

Three things came out of the sources that were read. A 2024 law-firm update says the Federal Trade Commission's Telemarketing Sales Rule was amended so that its ban on false or misleading statements now applies to business-to-business calls as well as consumer calls. This matters because most Resona customers are businesses, and the simple rule that follows is that everything you say on a sales call must be true.

A compliance-software vendor's page states that business-to-business marketing is not exempt from all rules. It says that sending marketing calls or texts to a mobile number using an automatic dialing system generally requires the recipient's prior express written consent, whether the recipient is a business or a consumer. The vendor sells compliance products, and the page didn't say who qualifies, how the rules apply to small business owners' personal mobile numbers, or what the penalties are. Finally, the details of Do Not Call rules for business numbers weren't clear in the material that was read.

Until management sets a written rule, our practice is as follows.

- Use only company-approved tools and templates for calls, texts, and emails.
- Do not send marketing texts to a mobile number unless the person has given consent through an approved process.
- Do not use automated dialing or messaging tools unless they are company-approved.
- Honor "stop," "unsubscribe," and "do not contact" immediately, and record it.
- Ask your leader whenever you are unsure.

A written contact policy covering calls, texts, and email, including consent and opt-outs, is an open item for management and counsel.

### Chunk 6: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Opening** | The first moments of a conversation, when you say who you are and why you are there |
| **Permission-based opening** | An opening that asks whether the person has time to talk |
| **Rapport** | The comfortable sense that the other person is on your side |
| **Active listening** | A way of listening fully and showing you heard, by pausing, saying back, and following up |
| **Mirroring** | The matching of another person's gestures or speech, whether naturally or on purpose |
| **Check-in** | A short question that asks how the person is doing with the conversation |
| **Soft no** | A polite, vague way of declining |
| **Gatekeeper** | A person who controls access to the decision-maker |
| **Consent (for contact)** | A person's agreement to be contacted in a particular way |
| **Opt-out** | A person's request to stop being contacted |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What should you check before approaching an owner in person?
> 2. What is the rule that applies to everything you say on a sales call?
> 3. A coworker wants to send a marketing text through a tool you haven't seen before. What does our current practice say to do?

> **Takeaway**
> Each setting asks for a slightly different approach, but respecting the person's time, following the contact rules, and saying only what is true apply to all of them.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before each contact, ask whether this is the right moment and the right channel for this person, and if you're not sure, ask first.
