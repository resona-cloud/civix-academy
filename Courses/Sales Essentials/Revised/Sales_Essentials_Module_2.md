## Lesson 2.1: Know What You Offer

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe who a product helps and who it does not, separate claims you can prove from claims you cannot, and say "I don't know" the right way.

### Chunk 1: Why product knowledge is a trust issue

Trust grows from credibility, and credibility comes from knowing your subject and being accurate about it. A salesperson who guesses, exaggerates, or contradicts themselves loses a small business owner quickly, and that owner may tell other owners.

You don't need to know everything. What you need to know is what is true, what isn't, and what you would have to check.

### Chunk 2: Five things to know cold

| Know this | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What it does,** in one or two plain sentences | A customer should understand it without jargon |
| **Who it helps most** | Shows where it truly fits |
| **Who it does not help** | Lets you say "this probably isn't right for you" with confidence |
| **What it costs and what is included,** including terms, limits, and anything extra | No surprises later |
| **What happens after the purchase:** onboarding, support, what the customer has to do | Sets honest expectations |

### Chunk 3: Who it fits and who it does not

Every product has a best-fit customer and customers it won't serve well. Sellers sometimes avoid thinking about the second group, yet it matters for three reasons. It lets you qualify politely and not waste the customer's time, it protects against refunds, complaints, and bad reviews from customers who were never a fit, and it makes your "yes, this fits" more believable.

A venture investor's article on selling argues that spotting poor-fit customers early saves time for everyone and builds trust. The article is practitioner opinion with no data behind that claim, and it's written about larger business sales, so the idea carries over better than its specifics do.

For your own preparation, complete two sentences for the product you sell, drawing the answers from your trainers and product materials and not from this course: "This is a good fit for people who ___," and "This is probably not a good fit for people who ___."

### Chunk 4: The claims inventory

Every statement you make about a product is either provable or it isn't. Before you talk to customers, sort what you say into three groups.

| Group | What it is | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Provable and current** | Features you can show, terms in writing, results the company can document | Say it plainly |
| **True for some, not all** | A result a few customers had; an example | Say it as an example and not as the norm ("one owner told us...") |
| **Not provable or not sure** | A hope, a guess, something you heard | Do not say it as fact. Say "I'm not sure, let me check" |

This follows the federal government's guidance on endorsements and claims, which says that statements must be truthful, that claims needing proof must have it, and that an unusual result shouldn't be presented as typical. That summary comes from the FTC's public endorsement-guidance page and isn't legal advice. Module 5 covers claims in more detail.

### Chunk 5: Saying "I don't know"

Customers forgive not knowing, but they don't forgive being misled. A good answer sounds like this:

> "That's a good question and I don't want to guess. Let me find out and email you the answer by tomorrow afternoon."

The follow-through matters as much as the answer: do it, and do it early. This one habit does more for your credibility than any amount of memorized product detail.

### Chunk 6: Know the limits of your authority

Know in advance what you can and cannot offer. Pricing is set in advance as a subscription plan, so there is no price to negotiate. You explain the published plans and any official promotions, and that is all. Custom terms, special promises, and timelines you cannot confirm are not yours to offer, and if you are not sure, you do not promise; you say you will check.

A fixed price is an advantage, because it removes haggling and lets you be completely clear about cost from the first conversation. It also means that a customer who asks "can you do better on price?" gets a straight answer and not a maybe, a situation Module 6 covers under price objections.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two things you should know about a product other than what it does.
> 2. Why should you know who a product does not fit?
> 3. A customer asks whether the product integrates with software you've never heard of. What do you say, and what do you do afterward?

> **Takeaway**
> Knowing what is true, who the product fits, and where it falls short lets you answer with confidence, and saying so plainly when you're unsure is what keeps that confidence believable.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before each conversation, write down the one claim you're most tempted to stretch, and decide now how you'll say it truthfully.

## Lesson 2.2: Know the Person

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe what is useful to learn about a person before contact, how much time to spend, and what to leave alone.

### Chunk 1: Why prepare at all

A few minutes of preparation lets you ask better questions and avoid wasting a busy owner's time. It also prevents mistakes such as asking something they have already answered or pitching something that obviously doesn't apply.

Preparation has limits, though. It isn't a way to appear to know more than you do, and it isn't a license to dig into someone's private life.

### Chunk 2: What is useful to learn

For a small business owner, the helpful basics usually are these.

| Topic | Examples |
|---|---|
| **What the business does** | Type of business, what they sell, who they serve |
| **Size and shape** | Rough size, one location or several, owner-run or staffed |
| **Anything recent and relevant** | New location, new hire, a seasonal rush, a recent announcement they made publicly |
| **Past contact with us** | Earlier conversations, notes, what they said they wanted, any promises we made |
| **Their role** | Owner, manager, employee (and who makes the decision) |

Start with your own notes, in the CRM or other records, because earlier conversations are the most valuable source and the one most often skipped. After that, check public business information: the business's own website, public listings, and public reviews. If you're meeting in person, you can also notice things at the location itself, such as signage, hours, and how busy it is.

### Chunk 3: How long to spend

A lead-generation agency's blog suggests a short time box of about five to ten minutes for small businesses, with the first ten to fifteen minutes giving most of the value for any prospect. That agency sells outreach services, and the numbers are its own rule of thumb and not research. The principle matters more than the numbers: stop when you have two or three useful points you'll actually use, because information you won't use is just trivia.

### Chunk 4: Use it to ask, not to impress

The same source suggests using what you learn to ask sharper questions and not to show off. Compare the two approaches.

> **Showing off:** "I saw you opened a second location in March and your reviews mention slow service."
>
> **Asking:** "I understand you recently opened a second location. How is that going so far?"

The second version invites the owner to tell their story, whereas the first can feel like surveillance.

### Chunk 5: What to leave alone

Keep to professional, business-relevant, publicly shared information. These are firm limits.

- Do not search for or mention personal details (family, home, health, personal social media, finances) that the person has not shared with you or made public for business purposes.
- Do not bring up information that a third party told you in confidence.
- Do not collect or store personal information you do not need.
- Do not pretend to a connection you do not have.
- Do not use information obtained in a way you would not want to explain.

If something personal comes up on its own, such as a customer telling you about their family, listen warmly and do not use it to pressure them. You can note what they chose to share for the next conversation, such as "mentioned a daughter starting school in the fall," if it's the kind of thing a friendly acquaintance would remember.

Resona keeps customer information in the company database, limited to non-sensitive information, and purges it after 365 days of no subscription. Keep to that standard by collecting only what you need to help the customer, and do not ask for or record anything sensitive. If a customer volunteers something sensitive, do not write it down, and tell your leader if you are unsure how to handle it.

Privacy and data rules vary by place, and counsel may want to confirm the retention rule and the wording customers are given.

### Chunk 6: Write it down

After each contact, write a short note covering what they said they want, what concerns they raised, what you promised, and the next step with a date. Notes are what make the next conversation feel like a continuation and not a restart.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is usually the most valuable thing to check before contacting someone again?
> 2. You've found that a prospect recently opened a second location. How would you use that in your opening, and why not simply mention everything you found?
> 3. Name two things to leave alone when preparing.

> **Takeaway**
> A few minutes of focused, respectful preparation helps you ask better questions, provided you stick to business-relevant, public information.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you go in, pick two facts you'll use and one question that grows from them, and leave the rest.

## Lesson 2.3: Set a Goal for the Conversation

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can write a specific goal for a conversation, explain why the goal is usually a next step and not a sale, and use a one-minute plan.

### Chunk 1: Why a goal helps

A sales trainer's open textbook says that without a goal set before the call, it's hard to know what to say, and that clear goals make a salesperson more confident and organized while respecting the customer's time. A goal also lets you tell afterward whether the conversation worked.

### Chunk 2: Success is not always a sale

For many small purchases, a decision can happen in one conversation, but often it doesn't, and that's fine. The same textbook notes that a first call may aim to build trust, learn how the person decides, or find out about their current situation, and that "success doesn't always mean closing." It also warns that overreaching, such as trying to close a major sale on the first call, sets you up for failure.

One way to think about it is in three levels.

| Goal level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Minimum:** the least that would make this conversation worthwhile | "Learn the owner's top frustration and permission to follow up." |
| **Target:** what you realistically want | "Agree on a short follow-up conversation with a specific date." |
| **Stretch:** a bonus if it goes very well | "The owner decides to start today." |

The three levels are a teaching device for this course and don't come from the textbook. The advice to set a realistic, specific goal does.

### Chunk 3: Make the goal specific

The textbook gives a set of qualities for a good objective, often called **SMART**: it's specific, which means it names the action or information, and measurable, so you can tell afterward whether you got there. It's also actionable, because you can influence it, realistic for the stage you're at, and time-bound, with a "by when."

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "Find out about their business" | "By the end of the conversation, learn what takes up most of the owner's week and what they have tried before." |
| "Make a sale" | "Agree on one next step with a date." |
| "Build rapport" | "Learn one thing about the owner that is not about our product, and use it in the follow-up." |

According to the same source, writing the goal down helps.

### Chunk 4: The goal belongs to the customer too

A goal is a direction and not a trap, so if the customer's needs lead somewhere else, follow them. A conversation that ends with "this isn't a fit" and mutual respect has still met a good goal, because you learned something true and the person's time wasn't wasted.

### Chunk 5: A one-minute plan

Before each conversation, write four lines. The first covers who you're talking to and what you know, which should be two or three facts. The second is your goal at the minimum, target, and stretch levels. The third is your first question, an opening that invites them to talk, and the fourth is the commitment you'll ask for at the end, which should be a specific next step.

Here is an example, which is fictional.

> **Who:** Marco runs a three-person plumbing shop. Opened a second truck this spring. Spoke once; he said scheduling is a headache.
> **Goal:** Minimum: learn what he's using now. Target: book a 20-minute follow-up this week. Stretch: he signs up today.
> **First question:** "Last time you mentioned scheduling is a headache. What's the hardest part this month?"
> **Ask at the end:** "Can I send you a short walk-through and call Thursday at 3?"

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Why might the best goal be a next step instead of a sale?
> 2. Rewrite this goal to be specific: "Find out about their business."
> 3. You're about to call a prospect for the first time. Write the first two lines of your one-minute plan.

> **Takeaway**
> A specific, realistic goal, usually a next step, keeps a conversation focused, and following the customer when they lead somewhere else is part of meeting it.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Write your goal on paper before you walk in or dial, and when the conversation ends, check it and note what you learned.

## Lesson 2.4: Your Own Readiness

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe how nerves and avoidance show up in selling, use practical habits to get started, and know when to ask for help.

### Chunk 1: Everyone feels it

Talking with strangers about buying is uncomfortable for many people, including experienced salespeople. Feeling nervous doesn't mean you're unsuited to the work; it means you're doing something that matters to you.

### Chunk 2: When nerves become avoidance

A research report from Baylor University's Keller Center (2010) looked at **sales call reluctance**, which is emotional discomfort that gets in the way of reaching out to enough people. It surveyed 3,319 real estate agents and described twelve ways the discomfort shows up, including avoiding calls to people you know, hesitating to use the phone, over-preparing instead of calling, resisting coaching, and waiting for the "right moment." Agents with higher reluctance scores made fewer prospecting contacts the previous week, and the report's authors suggest that awareness alone may be enough for many people.

The source needs care. The authors sell the assessment used in the survey and related books, the study covered one profession (real estate) in 2010, and it shows association and not cause. This lesson uses it only for the list of avoidance behaviors, which many people recognize in themselves.

### Chunk 3: Spot your own pattern

| If you notice yourself... | It may be... | Try... |
|---|---|---|
| Polishing notes or materials instead of reaching out | Over-preparing | Set a timer; when it ends, make the first contact |
| Waiting for a better moment | Avoiding | Pick a fixed time and protect it |
| Skipping people you know | Social discomfort | Start with a simple, honest conversation, not a pitch |
| Dreading the worst outcome | Expecting rejection | Remember the likely outcome is "not now," not an insult |
| Needing every answer first | Fear of looking unprepared | Practice saying "let me find out" |

### Chunk 4: Practical habits that help

Starting small makes the first contact easy, since a short, friendly opener asks little of you. A fixed block of time for outreach, protected like an appointment, takes away the daily decision about when to begin, and saying your opener out loud once or twice beforehand warms you up. A modest, specific goal is also easier to start than a large one.

Reframing a no helps as well, because a no is information: it usually means "not this, not now," and it tells you what to adjust. It's better to review after a batch of contacts than during it, so make three contacts and then look at what you learned. Looking after the basics matters too, meaning sleep, food, and a short break between conversations, and talking with a teammate or leader who has been through it can make the whole thing feel more ordinary.

### Chunk 5: When to ask for help

Ask your leader or trainer if you keep avoiding contact despite wanting to reach out, or if you dread it so much that it's affecting your sleep or mood. The same applies if you feel pressure to cross a line to hit a number, or if you're unsure what to say and have no one to practice with.

Asking for help is a normal part of learning the job. If stress or anxiety feels overwhelming, a doctor or counselor is the right place to turn, and it's fine to say so to your leader.

### Chunk 6: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Claims inventory** | A sorting of what you say about a product into provable, true-for-some, and not provable |
| **Best-fit customer** | A person whom the product helps well |
| **Poor-fit customer** | A person whom the product is unlikely to serve well |
| **Time box** | A fixed, short amount of time set aside for a task |
| **Pre-call objective** | A goal set before a conversation |
| **SMART goal** | A goal that is specific, measurable, actionable, realistic, and time-bound |
| **Minimum / target / stretch goal** | Three levels of what you want from a conversation |
| **One-minute plan** | A four-line note written before each conversation that covers who, goal, first question, and ask |
| **Sales call reluctance** | Emotional discomfort that interferes with reaching out to prospects |
| **Qualify** | To find out, politely, whether the product fits the person |
| **Authority (limits of)** | The range of what you are allowed to offer or promise on behalf of the company |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two ways avoidance can show up.
> 2. You notice you've spent the morning reorganizing your notes instead of calling anyone. What is this likely to be, and what is one habit that helps?
> 3. When should you ask a leader for help?

> **Takeaway**
> Nerves are normal, and starting small, protecting outreach time, and asking for help early keep them from turning into avoidance.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before your first contact of the day, say your opener aloud once, and then make the contact before you can talk yourself out of it.
