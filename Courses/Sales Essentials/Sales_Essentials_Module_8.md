# Sales Essentials
## Module 8: Follow-Up, Referrals and Professional Conduct

**Audience:** Everyone at Resona who talks with people about buying
**Prerequisite:** Sales Essentials Modules 1 to 7
**Lessons:** 8.1 Follow-Up That Helps · 8.2 Records and Habits · 8.3 Referrals and Reputation · 8.4 Resilience and Ethics
**Who "the customer" is:** Individual decision-makers, mostly small business owners and their staff.
**Tone:** Plain language. Honest selling. Fictional examples. Product-neutral.
**Pricing note:** Pricing is set in advance as a subscription. Reps explain published plans and any official promotions. They do not negotiate or invent prices.

## Lesson 8.1: Follow-Up That Helps

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can follow up in a way that is useful and respectful, and know the contact rules that apply to email, calls, and texts.

*Contact rules in this lesson are general education, not legal advice. Counsel and management decide the written policy. When unsure, ask before you send.*

### Chunk 1: Why follow up

Most decisions do not happen in one conversation. People get busy, need to check with someone, or forget. A good follow-up keeps the conversation alive **only if the person still wants it.** Follow-up is a service when it helps and a nuisance when it does not.

### Chunk 2: Make every contact useful

A sales-software blog (HubSpot, updated April 2025) advises against empty messages like "just checking in." It suggests each follow-up do one of four things:

| Purpose | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| **Restate the value** they cared about | "You said missed bookings were your biggest headache. Here's how the plan handles that." |
| **Share something useful** | "You asked about setup time. Here's what the first week looks like." |
| **Answer an open question** | "You asked about [topic]. Here's the answer I promised." |
| **Share relevant news** | "Something changed that matters for your plan: [fact]." |

That blog is vendor content, and its numbers, such as its "seven or more touches" claim, are weakly sourced. **This course does not set a number of touches.** The right number is the number the person welcomes.

### Chunk 3: Keep your promises

If you said "I'll send that Thursday," send it Thursday. Reliability is part of trust (Module 1.2). If you cannot, say so before the deadline.

### Chunk 4: The contact rules, in plain terms

Several laws govern how we may contact people. Here is what the sources say, in simple form.

| Channel | What to know | Source status |
|---|---|---|
| **Email** | The CAN-SPAM Act covers commercial email, **including email to businesses.** Requirements include accurate sender information, non-deceptive subject lines, a clear statement that it is an ad, a valid postal address, and a clear way to opt out. Opt-outs must be honored within **10 business days.** | FTC compliance guide (last updated July 2025) |
| **Phone calls** | The FTC's 2024 amendment to the Telemarketing Sales Rule extended its **ban on false or misleading statements** to calls to businesses, effective May 16, 2024. The rule text I read shows the proposal did not extend other provisions, such as Do Not Call Registry rules, to business calls. | Federal Register summary; counsel should confirm the final scope |
| **Texts** | Federal law (the TCPA) restricts marketing texts and autodialed or prerecorded calls to cell phones without the proper consent. In 2025, a court struck down the FCC's stricter "one-to-one" consent rule, and the FCC restored the earlier standard, which requires written consent with clear disclosures. | Law-firm articles; counsel should confirm |

**Rules of thumb that are safe anywhere:**

- **Never contact someone who asked you to stop** (Module 6.4). Record the request.
- **Do not text** a customer for marketing unless the company's written policy says how consent is obtained and recorded.
- **Be truthful** in every message. False or misleading statements are against the rules in any channel, including to businesses.
- **Use only company-approved email tools** that include the required opt-out and address.
- **One person's yes is not a blanket yes.** Permission to email about a topic is not permission to text.

### Chunk 5: How much is too much

A calm guide for this course:

- **Match their pace.** If they reply quickly, you can too.
- **If there is no reply,** a couple of useful, spaced-out messages are reasonable. Then ask once whether they would like you to stop (Module 6.4).
- **Never** send repeated messages that read as pressure.
- **Stop at "no."**

### Chunk 6: A short fictional exchange

> **Jordan** *(email, three days after a demo)*: "Hi Lena, you asked how the front desk would handle walk-ins. Here's a two-minute walkthrough. If this isn't the right time, tell me and I'll stop. Otherwise I'll check back Thursday as we discussed."

The message answers a question, gives a clear next step, and offers an easy way out.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two purposes a good follow-up can serve.
> 2. Does CAN-SPAM cover email to businesses?
> 3. A prospect says "stop emailing me." What do you do?

> **Takeaway**
> Follow up to help, not to nag. Keep promises, follow the contact rules, and stop when asked.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you send a follow-up, ask: "What does this give them?" If nothing, do not send it.

## Lesson 8.2: Records and Habits

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can keep short, accurate, non-sensitive notes, and build daily habits that make follow-up reliable.

### Chunk 1: Why keep notes

You cannot remember every conversation, and the customer expects you to. Notes let you pick up where you left off, keep promises, and pass the customer smoothly to a colleague.

### Chunk 2: What to record

Keep it short and factual:

| Record | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| **Who and when** | "Marco, plumbing business; call on [date]" |
| **What they care about** | "Wants fewer missed calls; two techs" |
| **What you promised** | "Send setup overview by Thursday" |
| **The next step and date** | "Follow-up call Friday at 10" |
| **Contact permissions** | "Email OK. Asked not to be texted." |
| **Objections and what helped** | "Worried about staff adoption; demo helped" |

### Chunk 3: What not to record

Resona keeps customer information in the company database, **non-sensitive only,** and purges it after **365 days** of no subscription (Module 2.2). So:

- **Do not record** anything sensitive: financial account details, health information, personal opinions about the person, or anything you would not be comfortable showing the customer.
- **Do not keep personal copies** in your own phone or notebook beyond what the company allows.
- **Stick to what you need.** If unsure, ask your leader.
- **Write notes as if the customer might read them.** That is a good test.

### Chunk 4: Daily habits

Small habits do more than big efforts:

1. **Start with a short plan** (Module 2.3): who to contact, what to prepare
2. **Log notes right after each conversation,** while you remember
3. **Set your next step and date** before you move on
4. **Check your promises list** at the end of the day
5. **Review once a week:** what worked, what did not, what to change

### Chunk 5: Handing off well

If a customer moves to another person at Resona (for support, setup, or another rep), **brief that person** so the customer does not have to repeat everything. Share what you promised and what matters to them.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three things to record after a conversation.
> 2. Name two things you must not record.
> 3. What is a good test for whether a note is appropriate?

> **Takeaway**
> Short, accurate, non-sensitive notes make follow-up reliable and fair.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> End every conversation by writing the next step and its date.

## Lesson 8.3: Referrals and Reputation

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can earn referrals honestly, handle reviews and testimonials correctly, and protect Resona's reputation.

### Chunk 1: Reputation is earned

Customers who are treated well talk about it. Customers who are treated badly talk about it more. The best referral program is **doing what you said, clearly and kindly.**

### Chunk 2: Asking for a referral

The open textbook (*Selling for Success*, NSCC) treats the sale as part of an ongoing relationship. Referrals come from that relationship. A good time to ask is **after the customer has experienced value,** not right after the yes (Module 7.4).

> "I'm glad it's working well. If you know another owner who might find this useful, I'd be glad to help them too. No pressure at all."

Do:
- **Wait until they are happy.**
- **Make it easy to say no.**
- **Accept the answer.**
- **Thank them,** whether or not they refer anyone.

### Chunk 3: Rewards and incentives

**Do not invent referral rewards.** If the company has an approved referral program, follow its written terms exactly. Otherwise, do not promise rewards, discounts, or gifts for referrals. Pricing and promotions are fixed (Module 1.4).

The FTC's rules on endorsements and consumer reviews matter here (see also Module 5.3). According to a law-firm summary (BCLP, September 2024):

- **A reward cannot depend on the reviewer giving a positive review** or a particular view, directly or by implication.
- **Incentivized reviews are not banned,** but a "material connection" such as a payment, free product, or prize must be **clearly disclosed** where people would not expect it.

Practical rules for this company:

- **Never offer anything in exchange for a good review.** You may ask for honest feedback.
- **Never write or edit a review or testimonial for a customer** or post one under another name.
- **Use a customer's name, quote, or business only with their permission,** and only through the approved process (Module 5.2).
- **Follow the written reviews policy** once the company has one (carried-over open item).

### Chunk 4: When someone is not happy

Unhappy customers are information. **Respond quickly, honestly, and kindly.** Do not argue, delete, or hide complaints. Pass problems to the right team. A complaint handled well often turns into loyalty.

If a customer posts a public complaint, **do not reply on your own.** Tell your leader. The company decides how to respond.

### Chunk 5: Recommending others

If a customer is not a good fit (Module 4.4), say so. **Do not recommend a specific competitor or company** unless Resona has approved referrals for that case. You can say, "I don't think we're the right fit, and I'd rather be honest than sell you something that doesn't help."

### Chunk 6: Protecting the company's name

Everything you say can reflect on Resona:

- **Be accurate** about what you are authorized to say (Module 1.4)
- **Be the same person** online and in person
- **Do not disparage** competitors or anyone else
- **If you make a mistake,** own it, fix it, and tell your leader

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. When is a good time to ask for a referral?
> 2. Name two things you must never do about reviews or testimonials.
> 3. A customer posts a public complaint. What do you do?

> **Takeaway**
> Earn referrals by doing right, ask without pressure, and never buy, write, or hide opinions.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> When a customer is happy, thank them first. Ask for a referral only if the moment is right, and make it easy to decline.

## Lesson 8.4: Resilience and Ethics

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can handle setbacks without losing your standards, and apply a simple test when a situation feels unclear.

### Chunk 1: Setbacks are normal

The open textbook says refusals are a normal part of selling, that success often takes several attempts, and that confidence and the right mental attitude help you keep going. It also advises recording what works and what does not. (It gives a statistic about how often prospects say no, but with no method or sample, so this course does not repeat it.)

Plain version: **most conversations will not end in a yes, and that does not mean you did something wrong.**

### Chunk 2: Resilience habits

| Habit | How |
|---|---|
| **Separate the no from yourself** | "That was a no to the offer, not to me." |
| **Learn one thing** | Write one line: what would I do differently? |
| **Reset between contacts** | Walk, breathe, or talk to a teammate |
| **Stay with your process** | Plan, ask, listen, follow up (Modules 2 to 8) |
| **Ask for help early** | Tell your leader if you are discouraged |

If a hard stretch lasts, talk to your leader. Needing support is normal, not weakness.

### Chunk 3: Pressure to cut corners

Slumps create temptation: overstate a benefit, skip a disclosure, keep contacting someone who said stop. **These shortcuts cost more than they gain.** They harm customers, they harm Resona, and they can create legal exposure. The standards from Module 1 do not relax when results do.

### Chunk 4: A simple test

When a situation feels unclear, ask four questions:

1. **Is it true?** Would I be comfortable if the customer knew everything I knew?
2. **Is it fair?** Am I helping them decide, or pushing them past a concern?
3. **Is it allowed?** Do I have authority and a written policy for this?
4. **Would I be comfortable if my leader and the customer saw it?**

If any answer is no or unsure, **stop and ask.**

### Chunk 5: Who to ask

Bring unclear situations to your leader. Examples that must always be escalated:

- A request for **special terms, refunds, or exceptions**
- A **legal question** from a customer
- A **complaint or threat** of a complaint
- A request to **stop contact** that you are unsure how to record
- A **mistake** you made or noticed

Asking is part of the job.

### Chunk 6: Putting the course together

The course in one line per module:

| Module | Habit |
|---|---|
| **1. What selling is** | Help people decide, honestly |
| **2. Preparing** | Know your product, your customer, your plan |
| **3. Opening and rapport** | Be clear, warm, and respectful of their time |
| **4. Discovery** | Ask and listen before you present |
| **5. Presenting value** | Tie benefits to what they said, and prove it truthfully |
| **6. Objections** | Treat concerns as questions; respect a no |
| **7. Closing** | Agree on a next step; no surprises on terms |
| **8. Follow-up and conduct** | Be useful, be reliable, and keep your standards |

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Follow-up** | Contact after a conversation that helps the customer move forward |
| **CAN-SPAM Act** | A federal law governing commercial email, including email to businesses |
| **Opt-out** | A person's request not to receive further marketing contact |
| **Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR)** | An FTC rule on sales calls; since 2024 its ban on misleading statements also covers calls to businesses |
| **TCPA** | A federal law restricting certain marketing calls and texts without proper consent |
| **Consent** | A person's clear, recorded permission to be contacted in a given way |
| **Referral** | A customer introducing someone else who may benefit |
| **Material connection** | A payment, gift, or other relationship that could affect how people view an endorsement or review |
| **Testimonial** | A customer's own statement about their experience, used with permission |
| **Handoff** | Passing a customer to a colleague with the context they need |
| **Resilience** | Continuing steadily after setbacks without lowering standards |
| **Escalate** | Bring a question or problem to your leader or the right team |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two resilience habits.
> 2. What are the four questions in the simple test?
> 3. Name two situations you must always escalate.

> **Takeaway**
> Keep your standards when results are hard. When unclear, ask four questions, then ask your leader.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After a hard conversation, write one thing you learned and one thing you did well.
