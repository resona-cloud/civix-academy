## Lesson 2.1: Know What You Offer

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe who a product helps and who it does not, separate claims you can prove from claims you cannot, and say "I don't know" the right way.

### Chunk 1: Why product knowledge is a trust issue

In Module 1 you learned that trust grows from credibility, and credibility comes from knowing your subject and being accurate about it. A salesperson who guesses, exaggerates, or contradicts themselves loses a small business owner quickly, and that owner may tell other owners.

You do not need to know everything. You need to know **what is true, what is not, and what you would have to check.**

### Chunk 2: Five things to know cold

| Know this | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What it does,** in one or two plain sentences | A customer should understand it without jargon |
| **Who it helps most** | Shows where it truly fits |
| **Who it does not help** | Lets you say "this probably isn't right for you" with confidence |
| **What it costs and what is included,** including terms, limits, and anything extra | No surprises later (Module 7.3) |
| **What happens after the purchase:** onboarding, support, what the customer has to do | Sets honest expectations |

### Chunk 3: Who it fits and who it does not

Every product has a best-fit customer and customers it will not serve well. Sellers sometimes avoid thinking about the second group, but it matters:

- It lets you **qualify politely** and not waste the customer's time (Module 4.4).
- It protects against **refunds, complaints, and bad reviews** from customers who were never a fit.
- It makes your "yes, this fits" more believable.

A venture investor's article on selling argues that spotting poor-fit customers early saves time for everyone and builds trust. The article is practitioner opinion with no data behind that claim, and it is written about larger business sales, so use the idea, not its specifics.

**Exercise for your own prep.** Complete two sentences for the product you sell (answers will come from your trainers and product materials, not from this course):

- "This is a good fit for people who ___."
- "This is probably not a good fit for people who ___."

### Chunk 4: The claims inventory

Every statement you make about a product is either **provable** or it is not. Before you talk to customers, sort what you say into three groups:

| Group | What it is | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Provable and current** | Features you can show, terms in writing, results the company can document | Say it plainly |
| **True for some, not all** | A result a few customers had; an example | Say it as an example, not as the norm ("one owner told us...") |
| **Not provable or not sure** | A hope, a guess, something you heard | Do not say it as fact. Say "I'm not sure, let me check" |

This follows the federal government's guidance on endorsements and claims: statements must be truthful, claims that need proof must have it, and an unusual result should not be presented as typical. *(From the FTC's endorsement guidance, a summary of its public page, not legal advice. Module 5.3 covers claims in more detail.)*

### Chunk 5: Saying "I don't know"

Customers forgive not knowing. They do not forgive being misled. A good answer sounds like this:

> "That's a good question and I don't want to guess. Let me find out and email you the answer by tomorrow afternoon."

Then do it, and do it early. This one habit does more for your credibility than any amount of memorized product detail.

### Chunk 6: Know the limits of your authority

Know in advance what you can and cannot offer. **Pricing is set in advance as a subscription plan,** so there is no price to negotiate. You explain the published plans and any official promotions, and that is all. Custom terms, special promises, and timelines you cannot confirm are not yours to offer. If you are not sure, you do not promise. You say you will check.

A fixed price is an advantage: it removes haggling and lets you be completely clear about cost from the first conversation. It also means a customer who says "can you do better on price?" gets a straight answer, not a maybe (Module 6.3 covers price objections).

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two things you should know about a product other than what it does.
> 2. Why should you know who a product does not fit?
> 3. What do you say when you do not know an answer?

> **Takeaway**
> Know what is true, who it fits, and where it falls short. When you are unsure, say so and follow up.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before each conversation, write down the one claim you are most tempted to stretch. Decide now how you will say it truthfully.

## Lesson 2.2: Know the Person

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe what is useful to learn about a person before contact, how much time to spend, and what to leave alone.

### Chunk 1: Why prepare at all

A few minutes of preparation lets you ask better questions and avoid wasting a busy owner's time. It also prevents mistakes such as asking something they have already answered or pitching something that obviously does not apply.

Preparation is **not** a way to appear to know more than you do, and it is not a license to dig into someone's private life.

### Chunk 2: What is useful to learn

For a small business owner, the helpful basics usually are:

| Topic | Examples |
|---|---|
| **What the business does** | Type of business, what they sell, who they serve |
| **Size and shape** | Rough size, one location or several, owner-run or staffed |
| **Anything recent and relevant** | New location, new hire, a seasonal rush, a recent announcement they made publicly |
| **Past contact with us** | Earlier conversations, notes, what they said they wanted, any promises we made |
| **Their role** | Owner, manager, employee (and who makes the decision) |

**Where to look.** Start with **your own notes** (CRM or records) because earlier conversations are the most valuable and most often skipped. Then check **public business information:** the business's own website, public listings, and public reviews. In person, you can also notice things at the location itself, such as signage, hours, and how busy it is.

### Chunk 3: How long to spend

A lead-generation agency's blog suggests a short time box: about **five to ten minutes for small businesses,** with the first ten to fifteen minutes giving most of the value for any prospect. *(That agency sells outreach services, and the numbers are its own rule of thumb, not research.)* The principle is what matters: **stop when you have two or three useful points you will actually use.** Information you will not use is just trivia.

### Chunk 4: Use it to ask, not to impress

The same source suggests using what you learn to ask sharper questions instead of showing off. Compare:

> **Showing off:** "I saw you opened a second location in March and your reviews mention slow service."
>
> **Asking:** "I understand you recently opened a second location. How is that going so far?"

The second invites the owner to tell their story. The first can feel like surveillance.

### Chunk 5: What to leave alone

Keep to **professional, business-relevant, publicly shared information.** Do not:

- Search for or mention **personal details** (family, home, health, personal social media, finances) that the person has not shared with you or made public for business purposes
- Bring up **information that a third party told you in confidence**
- **Collect or store** personal information you do not need
- Pretend to a connection you do not have
- Use information obtained in a way you would not want to explain

If something personal comes up on its own, such as a customer telling you about their family, listen warmly, and **do not use it to pressure.** You can note what they chose to share for the next conversation, such as "mentioned a daughter starting school in the fall," if it is the kind of thing a friendly acquaintance would remember.

**How Resona handles customer information.** Customer information is kept in the company database, and it is limited to non-sensitive information. It is purged after 365 days of no subscription. Keep to that standard: collect only what you need to help the customer, and do not ask for or record anything sensitive. If a customer volunteers something sensitive, do not write it down, and tell your leader if you are unsure how to handle it.

*(Privacy and data rules vary by place. Counsel may want to confirm the retention rule and the wording customers are given.)*

### Chunk 6: Write it down

After each contact, write a short note: what they said they want, what concerns they raised, what you promised, and the next step with a date (Module 8.2). Notes are what make the next conversation feel like a continuation instead of a restart.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is usually the most valuable thing to check before contacting someone again?
> 2. How should you use what you learn: to impress or to ask? Why?
> 3. Name two things to leave alone when preparing.

> **Takeaway**
> A few minutes of focused, respectful preparation helps you ask better questions. Stick to business-relevant, public information.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you go in, pick two facts you will use and one question that grows from them. Skip the rest.

## Lesson 2.3: Set a Goal for the Conversation

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can write a specific goal for a conversation, explain why the goal is usually a next step and not a sale, and use a one-minute plan.

### Chunk 1: Why a goal helps

A sales trainer's open textbook says that without a goal set before the call, it is hard to know what to say, and that clear goals make a salesperson more confident and organized while respecting the customer's time. A goal also lets you tell afterward whether the conversation worked.

### Chunk 2: Success is not always a sale

For many small purchases, a decision can happen in one conversation. Often it does not, and that is fine. The same textbook notes that a first call may aim to build trust, learn how the person decides, or find out about their current situation, and that "success doesn't always mean closing." It also warns that overreaching, such as trying to close a major sale on the first call, sets you up for failure.

A useful way to think about it:

| Goal level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Minimum:** the least that would make this conversation worthwhile | "Learn the owner's top frustration and permission to follow up." |
| **Target:** what you realistically want | "Agree on a short follow-up conversation with a specific date." |
| **Stretch:** a bonus if it goes very well | "The owner decides to start today." |

*(The three levels are my own teaching device. The open textbook does not use them. The advice to set a realistic, specific goal is from the textbook.)*

### Chunk 3: Make the goal specific

The textbook gives a set of qualities for a good objective, often called **SMART:**

- **Specific:** names the action or information
- **Measurable:** you can tell afterward whether you got there
- **Actionable:** you can influence it
- **Realistic:** achievable for this stage
- **Time-bound:** has a "by when"

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "Find out about their business" | "By the end of the conversation, learn what takes up most of the owner's week and what they have tried before." |
| "Make a sale" | "Agree on one next step with a date." |
| "Build rapport" | "Learn one thing about the owner that is not about our product, and use it in the follow-up." |

Writing the goal down helps, according to the same source.

### Chunk 4: The goal belongs to the customer too

A goal is not a trap. It is a direction. **If the customer's needs lead somewhere else, follow them.** A conversation that ends with "this isn't a fit" and mutual respect has still met a good goal: you learned something true, and the person was not wasted time.

### Chunk 5: A one-minute plan

Before each conversation, write four lines:

1. **Who** am I talking to, and what do I know? (Two or three facts)
2. **My goal:** minimum, target, stretch
3. **My first question:** an opening question that invites them to talk (Module 4)
4. **The commitment I will ask for at the end:** a specific next step (Module 7)

Example (fictional):

> **Who:** Marco runs a three-person plumbing shop. Opened a second truck this spring. Spoke once; he said scheduling is a headache.
> **Goal:** *Minimum:* learn what he's using now. *Target:* book a 20-minute follow-up this week. *Stretch:* he signs up today.
> **First question:** "Last time you mentioned scheduling is a headache. What's the hardest part this month?"
> **Ask at the end:** "Can I send you a short walk-through and call Thursday at 3?"

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Why might the best goal be a next step instead of a sale?
> 2. Rewrite this goal to be specific: "Find out about their business."
> 3. What are the four lines of the one-minute plan?

> **Takeaway**
> Set a specific, realistic goal, usually a next step. Follow the customer if they lead somewhere else.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Write your goal on paper before you walk in or dial. Check it when the conversation ends, and note what you learned.

## Lesson 2.4: Your Own Readiness

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe how nerves and avoidance show up in selling, use practical habits to get started, and know when to ask for help.

### Chunk 1: Everyone feels it

Talking with strangers about buying is uncomfortable for many people, including experienced salespeople. Feeling nervous does not mean you are not suited to it. It means you are doing something that matters to you.

### Chunk 2: When nerves become avoidance

A research report from Baylor University's Keller Center (2010) looked at **sales call reluctance:** emotional discomfort that gets in the way of reaching out to enough people. It surveyed 3,319 real estate agents and described twelve ways the discomfort shows up, including **avoiding calls to people you know, hesitating to use the phone, over-preparing instead of calling, resisting coaching, and waiting for the "right moment."** Agents with higher reluctance scores made fewer prospecting contacts the previous week. The report's authors suggest that **awareness alone may be enough for many people.**

**Be careful with this source.** The authors sell the assessment used in the survey and related books. The study was in one profession (real estate), was from 2010, and shows association, not cause. I use it only for the list of avoidance behaviors, which many people recognize in themselves.

### Chunk 3: Spot your own pattern

| If you notice yourself... | It may be... | Try... |
|---|---|---|
| Polishing notes or materials instead of reaching out | Over-preparing | Set a timer; when it ends, make the first contact |
| Waiting for a better moment | Avoiding | Pick a fixed time and protect it |
| Skipping people you know | Social discomfort | Start with a simple, honest conversation, not a pitch |
| Dreading the worst outcome | Expecting rejection | Remember the likely outcome is "not now," not an insult |
| Needing every answer first | Fear of looking unprepared | Practice "let me find out" (Lesson 2.1) |

### Chunk 4: Practical habits that help

- **Start small.** Make the first contact easy: a short, friendly opener.
- **Use a fixed block of time** for outreach, and protect it like an appointment.
- **Warm up.** Say your opener out loud once or twice before the first contact.
- **Keep your goal modest and specific** (Lesson 2.3). A small goal is easier to start.
- **Reframe a no.** A no is information. It usually means "not this, not now," and it tells you what to adjust (Module 6.4).
- **Review after, not during.** Make three contacts, then review what you learned.
- **Look after the basics:** sleep, food, and a short break between conversations.
- **Talk with a teammate or leader** who has been through it.

### Chunk 5: When to ask for help

Ask your leader or trainer if:

- You keep avoiding contact despite wanting to reach out
- You dread it so much that it is affecting your sleep or mood
- You feel pressure to cross a line to hit a number
- You are unsure what to say and have no one to practice with

Asking for help is a normal part of learning the job. *(If stress or anxiety feels overwhelming, a doctor or counselor is the right place to turn, and it is fine to say so to your leader.)*

### Chunk 6: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Claims inventory** | A sorting of what you say about a product into provable, true-for-some, and not provable |
| **Best-fit customer** | A person the product helps well |
| **Poor-fit customer** | A person the product is unlikely to serve well |
| **Time box** | A fixed, short amount of time set for a task |
| **Pre-call objective** | A goal set before a conversation |
| **SMART goal** | A goal that is specific, measurable, actionable, realistic, and time-bound |
| **Minimum / target / stretch goal** | Three levels of what you want from a conversation |
| **One-minute plan** | Four lines you write before each conversation: who, goal, first question, ask |
| **Sales call reluctance** | Emotional discomfort that interferes with reaching out to prospects |
| **Qualify** | To find out, politely, whether the product fits the person |
| **Authority (limits of)** | What you are allowed to offer or promise on behalf of the company |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two ways avoidance can show up.
> 2. Give two habits that make starting easier.
> 3. When should you ask a leader for help?

> **Takeaway**
> Nerves are normal. Start small, protect your outreach time, and ask for help early.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before your first contact of the day, say your opener aloud once. Then make the contact before you can talk yourself out of it.
