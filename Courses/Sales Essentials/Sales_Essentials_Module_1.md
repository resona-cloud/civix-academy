# Sales Essentials
## Module 1: What Selling Is (and Is Not)

**Audience:** Everyone at Resona who talks with people about buying
**Prerequisite:** Resona Foundations
**Lessons:** 1.1 Selling as Helping Someone Decide · 1.2 The Buyer's Journey · 1.3 Trust Is the Real Product · 1.4 Your Role and Its Boundaries
**Who "the customer" is in this course:** Individual decision-makers, mostly small business owners and their staff. Not teams, committees, or enterprise buyers (that is the High Yield Sales course).
**Tone:** Plain language. Honest selling. Fictional examples. Product-neutral.

## Lesson 1.1: Selling as Helping Someone Decide

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain what selling is in plain terms, describe how a seller-first conversation differs from a buyer-first one, and say why the buyer's problem comes before the product.

### Chunk 1: A working definition

**Selling is helping a person decide whether a product or service solves a problem they have.**

Three things follow from that definition:

- **The person has a problem or goal.** Without one, there is nothing to sell.
- **Deciding is their job, not yours.** You inform, you ask, you clarify. You do not decide for them.
- **"No" is a valid result.** If the product does not fit, a good conversation ends with a respectful no.

### Chunk 2: Two kinds of conversation

| | **Seller-first** | **Buyer-first** |
|---|---|---|
| **Starts with** | The product and its features | The person's situation and goal |
| **Talks about** | What we offer | What matters to them |
| **Questions are used to** | Steer toward a pitch | Understand the problem |
| **Ends when** | The seller gets a yes or gives up | The person has what they need to decide |
| **How the person feels** | Handled, rushed, or talked at | Heard and in control |

Small business owners are busy, and many do not enjoy being sold to. A 2014 survey by a business-coaching company, covering 401 owners (mostly with under 50 employees), found 57% said vendor information is "too sales-oriented," and that owners were more influenced by other owners using a product than by the vendor itself. *(That survey is old, from a single self-selected source, with no published method. Treat it as a hint about attitudes, not a measurement.)*

The practical takeaway does not depend on any statistic: **the less a conversation feels like a pitch, the more the person engages.**

### Chunk 3: Why the problem comes first

A product only has value in relation to a problem. Compare two openings with the same business owner, a fictional bakery owner named Dana:

> **Seller-first:** "Hi, I'm with a company that offers a tool with scheduling, reporting, and automated reminders. Let me walk you through the features."
>
> **Buyer-first:** "Hi Dana, I work with small shops like yours. Can I ask what takes up most of your time each week that you wish didn't?"

The first gives Dana a list to evaluate. The second gives Dana a reason to talk. **If Dana's real problem is late supplier orders, a scheduling tool is the wrong answer.** You can only find that out by asking.

### Chunk 4: What selling is not

- **Not convincing.** If you must talk someone into it, you may be selling the wrong thing to the wrong person.
- **Not tricking.** A sale built on a misunderstanding comes back as a refund, a complaint, or a bad review.
- **Not talking.** Strong sellers spend more time asking and listening than presenting.
- **Not a one-time event.** The sale is the start of a relationship, and the customer's experience afterward is part of the job (Module 7.4).

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. In your own words, what is selling?
> 2. Name two differences between a seller-first and a buyer-first conversation.
> 3. Why does the problem come before the product?

> **Takeaway**
> Selling is helping someone decide. Start with their problem, and accept that "no" is sometimes the right answer.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you say anything about the product, ask yourself: "Do I know what this person is trying to fix or achieve?" If not, ask first.

## Lesson 1.2: The Buyer's Journey

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe the three common stages of a buying decision, explain what a small business owner needs at each stage, and recognize where a conversation is likely to stall.

### Chunk 1: People do not buy in one step

Before anyone buys, they usually move through stages. A common model has three, and many sales trainers use some version of it:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Awareness<br/>Something is not working<br/>or could be better] --> B[Consideration<br/>What kinds of solutions exist?]
  B --> C[Decision<br/>Which one, and from whom?]
  C --> D[After the buy<br/>Does it work as promised?]
  D -.->|Word of mouth| A
```

The model is a simplification. A journal-style version would show people going back and forth, skipping steps, or stopping. One sales-software vendor's guide notes that real journeys are "winding" and not a straight funnel. **Treat the stages as a way to ask better questions, not as a track the customer must follow.**

### Chunk 2: What the person needs at each stage

| Stage | What is going on | What they need | What helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | They feel a problem or see an opportunity, but may not know their options or whom to trust | To understand the problem and that solutions exist | Clear explanation, credible examples, no pressure |
| **Consideration** | They are comparing kinds of solutions (for example, hire someone, use a tool, or do nothing) and worry about choosing wrong | Help comparing options and the tradeoffs | Honest comparisons, including cases where our product is not the best fit |
| **Decision** | They have a type of solution in mind and are choosing a specific one | Confidence that this choice will work for their situation | Evidence tied to their problem, clear terms, easy next steps |
| **After the buy** | They discover whether it works | Support and follow-through | Prompt help, expectations kept (Module 7.4) |

*(Stage names and the "what helps" column follow a Close (sales software vendor) article on the buyer's journey, adapted for this course. The article is vendor content, and its supporting statistics are not independently verified, so I did not use them.)*

### Chunk 3: A small business owner's version

Small business owners often:

- **Decide alone or with one other person,** such as a partner or key employee, so the journey can be fast
- **Wear many hats,** so time is the scarce resource
- **Weigh cash carefully,** since a purchase may come from money they feel directly
- **Trust other owners,** so referrals and reviews matter (the 2014 survey above found peers were the most-cited influence)
- **Need it to work on day one,** because they usually have no one to troubleshoot for them

This is why a short, clear, honest conversation tends to beat a long pitch.

### Chunk 4: Where conversations stall

| Sign | Likely stage problem | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm not sure we need this" | Awareness: the problem is not felt yet | Ask about their current situation (Module 4), not pitch more |
| "I'm looking at a few options" | Consideration: comparing | Offer an honest comparison and the questions they should ask any seller |
| "I need to think about it" | Decision: unresolved worry | Ask what they would want to be sure about (Module 6) |
| "Sounds good" followed by silence | Interest without commitment | Agree on a specific next step (Module 7) |
| "It's not what I expected" | After the buy: expectations missed | Listen, fix what you can, learn from it |

### Chunk 5: You do not control the journey

You cannot speed up someone's thinking. You can make each stage easier by being clear, honest, and available, and by not pushing the person past where they are.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name the three stages before a purchase.
> 2. What does a person in the Consideration stage need most?
> 3. Why is the model a simplification and not a rule?

> **Takeaway**
> People move through awareness, consideration, and decision at their own pace. Meet them where they are.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Ask yourself: "Which stage is this person in?" If you do not know, ask a question that would reveal it: "How long has this been on your mind?"

## Lesson 1.3: Trust Is the Real Product

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe four ingredients of trust, say which one salespeople most often get wrong, and list everyday habits that build or erode it.

### Chunk 1: Why trust comes first

Customers cannot fully inspect a product before buying. They rely on whether they trust the **person** and the **company** behind it. A small business owner who has been burned before is especially careful.

Trust is built from what you do, not from what you say about yourself. It builds slowly and can break quickly.

### Chunk 2: The Trust Equation

Consultants who wrote a well-known book on advisors proposed a simple model called the **Trust Equation.** It is a practitioner framework, not peer-reviewed research, and the firm that publishes it sells training. It is useful as a checklist:

**Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-Orientation**

| Ingredient | In plain words | In a sales conversation |
|---|---|---|
| **Credibility** | Do I believe what you say, and do you know your subject? | Accurate, specific answers; saying "I don't know, let me find out" |
| **Reliability** | Do you do what you say you will? | Showing up on time, sending what you promised, returning calls |
| **Intimacy** | Is it safe to tell you something sensitive? | Keeping confidences; not repeating what you hear to others |
| **Self-Orientation** | Whose interests are you focused on, theirs or yours? | The one that lowers trust the most when it is high |

The same source argues that **self-orientation is the factor that most often undermines sellers.** It sits in the denominator, so a high number shrinks everything else. A person can sound credible, show up on time, and keep confidences, but if the customer senses you care mostly about your commission, trust drops.

### Chunk 3: Signs of high and low self-orientation

| Looks like high self-orientation | Looks like low self-orientation |
|---|---|
| Steering every topic back to the product | Following the customer's concerns, even when they point away from a sale |
| Checking the clock or phone | Full attention |
| Interrupting | Letting them finish |
| Pushing past a hesitation | Asking what the hesitation is |
| Talking about your goals ("I'm trying to hit my number") | Talking about theirs |
| Exaggerating to close | Admitting limits |

### Chunk 4: Small habits that build trust

- **Be early,** or at least on time. If you will be late, say so before it happens.
- **Do what you said you would do,** by when you said you would do it.
- **Say "I don't know"** and then find out.
- **Put promises in writing** (an email, a text) so both people remember the same thing.
- **Admit mistakes quickly** and fix them.
- **Respect "no"** without a guilt trip (Module 6.4).
- **Keep what you hear private,** including what one customer tells you about their business.

### Chunk 5: Things that erode trust fast

- Overstating what the product does
- Hiding costs, terms, or limits
- Speaking badly about competitors
- Fake urgency ("this price ends tonight") when it is not true
- Contacting someone again and again after they said no
- Making a promise you cannot keep, even a small one

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name the four ingredients in the Trust Equation.
> 2. Which one is in the denominator, and what does that mean?
> 3. Give two habits that build trust and two that erode it.

> **Takeaway**
> Customers buy from people they trust. Credibility, reliability, and discretion help; focusing on yourself hurts.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you leave, check: "Did I promise anything? Is it written down? When will I do it?" Then do it early.

## Lesson 1.4: Your Role and Its Boundaries

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can state what you do and do not do in a sales conversation, list common pressure tactics to avoid and why, and describe when to escalate.

### Chunk 1: Your role

In a sales conversation you are a **guide and a source of accurate information.** You:

- **Ask** questions to understand the person's situation (Module 4)
- **Explain** what the product does and does not do, in plain language (Module 5)
- **Show** how it would fit their situation, with honest evidence
- **Answer** concerns truthfully (Module 6)
- **Offer** clear next steps and let the person decide (Module 7)
- **Follow through** on every commitment (Module 8)

### Chunk 2: What you never do

- **Overstate.** Do not claim results, features, or savings that are not true and provable.
- **Hide.** Do not leave out costs, terms, limits, or conditions the customer would want to know.
- **Pressure.** See the list below.
- **Promise outcomes.** No one can guarantee how a customer's business will do.
- **Give advice you are not qualified to give.** Legal, tax, financial, and accounting questions go to the customer's own professionals.
- **Misuse what you learn.** What a customer tells you about their business stays confidential.
- **Make commitments outside your authority.** Pricing is set in advance as a subscription plan, so you do not negotiate, invent, or promise a different price. You offer the published plans and any official promotions, and nothing else. Custom terms, special promises, and any request outside the published plans go to the right person at Resona.

### Chunk 3: Pressure tactics to avoid

A sales-software vendor's article lists tactics it calls high-pressure. The list is from a vendor blog and reflects that company's view, but the tactics are widely recognized as harmful to trust:

| Tactic | Why it backfires |
|---|---|
| **Fake or tight deadlines** ("price ends today") | Feels manipulative, and can be deceptive if the deadline is not real |
| **"Act now or miss out" language** | Rushes a decision the person is not ready to make |
| **Excessive contact** (repeated calls, texts, or emails) | Reads as desperate and can lead people to block you |
| **Irrelevant add-ons** | Signals you care about the total, not their need |
| **Attacking competitors** | Sounds petty, and false statements can create legal risk |
| **Ignoring a clear "no"** | Ends the relationship and harms reputation |
| **Guilt or passive-aggressive remarks** | Damages trust immediately |
| **Making it hard to cancel or opt out** | Turns a customer into an angry critic |

*(Source: a Close blog post, 2023, updated 2026. It also cites several statistics without methods, which I did not use.)*

**A simple test:** would you be comfortable if the customer saw exactly what you were thinking and why you said it? If not, don't do it.

### Chunk 4: When you are unsure

Stop and ask. Good moments to escalate:

- The customer asks for something outside what you can offer
- A claim or promise is being requested that you cannot verify
- The customer seems confused, upset, or vulnerable
- A customer raises a legal, billing, or safety concern
- You think you made a mistake

**Say:** "That's a good question. Let me check and get back to you by (day)." Then do it.

### Chunk 5: Why the boundaries help you

| If you stay in role... | ...then |
|---|---|
| Claims are true and provable | Customers stay, refunds drop, and referrals come |
| You avoid pressure | People return your calls |
| You escalate what is beyond you | Problems stay small |
| You keep confidences | Owners tell you what they really need |

**The long game:** small business owners talk to each other. A reputation for being straight with people travels and helps every conversation afterward.

### Chunk 6: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Selling** | Helping a person decide whether a product or service solves a problem they have |
| **Seller-first** | A conversation that starts with the product and the seller's goals |
| **Buyer-first** | A conversation that starts with the person's situation and goal |
| **Buyer's journey** | The stages a person moves through before and after a purchase |
| **Awareness** | The stage where a person recognizes a problem or opportunity |
| **Consideration** | The stage where a person compares kinds of solutions |
| **Decision** | The stage where a person chooses a specific option |
| **Trust Equation** | A practitioner framework: trust = (credibility + reliability + intimacy) ÷ self-orientation |
| **Self-orientation** | How much a person focuses on their own interests instead of the customer's |
| **High-pressure tactic** | A technique that rushes or pushes a person past their own judgment |
| **Escalate** | Bring a question or concern to the right person instead of guessing |
| **Provable claim** | A statement about a product or result that can be shown to be true |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three things you never do in a sales conversation.
> 2. Why is a fake deadline harmful?
> 3. What do you say when you are unsure of an answer?

> **Takeaway**
> You are a guide. Stay accurate, avoid pressure, keep promises, and escalate what is beyond you.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Keep a short note of every promise you make and when it is due. Check it before every follow-up.
