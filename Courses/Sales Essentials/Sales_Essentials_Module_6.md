# Sales Essentials
## Module 6: Objections

**Audience:** Everyone at Resona who talks with people about buying
**Prerequisite:** Sales Essentials Modules 1 to 5
**Lessons:** 6.1 What an Objection Really Is · 6.2 A Simple Process · 6.3 Common Objections · 6.4 Respecting a No
**Who "the customer" is:** Individual decision-makers, mostly small business owners and their staff.
**Tone:** Plain language. Honest selling. Fictional examples. Product-neutral.
**Pricing note:** Pricing is set in advance as a subscription. Reps explain published plans and any official promotions. They do not negotiate or invent prices.

## Lesson 6.1: What an Objection Really Is

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain what an objection is, see it as information and not an attack, and name common types.

### Chunk 1: Objections are normal and useful

An objection is **a concern, question, or hesitation a person raises about buying.** It is not rudeness. It usually means one of three things:

| It may be... | Example | What the person wants |
|---|---|---|
| **A question** | "How long does setup take?" | An answer |
| **A worry** | "What if my staff won't use it?" | Reassurance backed by facts |
| **A real no** | "We're not interested." | Respect (Lesson 6.4) |

People who are not interested rarely bother raising concerns. They just stop answering. An objection often means **they are still engaged and need something to decide.**

### Chunk 2: Treat it as a question

An open textbook on selling (*Selling for Success*, NSCC) lists six habits for handling almost any objection. The first and most useful is **to view the objection as a question, not a personal attack.** The others:

- **Respond with a question** to understand it
- **Restate the objection** before answering
- **Pause** before you respond
- **Use real examples and testimonials** where they fit (Module 5)
- **Never argue** with the person

The textbook draws on practitioner and trade sources, not on studies. It is a good checklist, not proof.

### Chunk 3: Six common types

The same textbook groups objections into six categories. They are a good starting map:

| Type | What it sounds like | What is often underneath |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** | "Will it work for my kind of shop?" | A doubt about fit or risk |
| **Source** | "I've never heard of your company." | A question about trust |
| **Price** | "That's more than I expected." | A doubt about value, or habit |
| **Money** | "Cash is tight right now." | A real limit on affordability or timing |
| **Already satisfied** | "We're fine with what we have." | Possibly a hidden concern, or no felt need |
| **"I need to think about it"** | "Let me get back to you." | A worry not yet spoken, or a soft no |

### Chunk 4: Hidden objections

The spoken objection may not be the real one. "It's too expensive" can mean "I don't believe it will work." "Let me think about it" can mean "I'm worried about X and I don't want to say." The next lesson shows how to ask kindly to find out.

### Chunk 5: What not to do

The textbook's cautions, which match common sense:

- **Do not argue,** call the person wrong, or get defensive
- **Do not criticize competitors** or your own company or product
- **Do not lie.** If you do not know, say so and follow up (Module 2.1)
- **Do not ignore an objection** or talk over it

And from this course: **do not use pressure to push past a concern** (Module 1.4).

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three things an objection may be.
> 2. Why can an objection be a sign of engagement?
> 3. Name two things you should not do when you hear one.

> **Takeaway**
> An objection is information. Treat it as a question, listen first, and never argue.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> When you hear a concern, take a breath and say to yourself, "That's a question." Then respond to the question.

## Lesson 6.2: A Simple Process

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can handle an objection using five steps: listen, acknowledge, explore, respond, and check.

### Chunk 1: The five steps

A common training model is called **LAER:** Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, Respond. It is taught by sales trainers and described on several consulting and training sites, none of which provide independent evidence that it works better than other approaches. It is a sensible structure. This course adds a fifth step, **Check,** because an answer is only useful if it landed.

| Step | What you do | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Listen** | Let them finish. Do not interrupt. Notice tone and feeling. | (Silence, nodding) |
| **2. Acknowledge** | Show you heard it. This is not agreeing. | "That's a fair concern." |
| **3. Explore** | Ask an open question to find the real issue. | "Can you tell me more about what worries you?" |
| **4. Respond** | Answer what the exploring showed, truthfully and briefly. | "Here's how setup works for most shops." |
| **5. Check** | Ask whether it answered the concern. | "Does that address it, or is there more?" |

### Chunk 2: Why explore before answering

The most common mistake is answering the first words, not the real concern. A consulting-site article on LAER warns about this: confidence that you already know the real concern should be treated with suspicion. (That article cites no data, so treat it as practical advice.)

> **Customer:** "That's too much for us."
> **Weak:** "But it saves you so much time!"
> **Better:** "Understood. When you say it's too much, is it the monthly cost, or are you not sure yet it would pay off?"

The answers lead to different responses. If it is cost, that is one conversation (Lesson 6.3). If it is doubt, you may need to show how it works.

### Chunk 3: Useful phrases

| Purpose | Try |
|---|---|
| **Acknowledge** | "That makes sense." "I'm glad you said that." "A lot of owners ask that." |
| **Restate** | "So you're worried your team won't use it. Have I got that right?" |
| **Explore** | "What would you need to see to feel comfortable?" "What's behind that?" |
| **Respond** | "Here's what I know..." "Here's what I don't know yet, and I'll find out." |
| **Check** | "Is that helpful?" "What else is on your mind?" |

### Chunk 4: Keep the pace calm

- **Pause before you answer.** The textbook advises this so you do not oversell.
- **Keep your voice level.** Defensiveness sounds like pressure.
- **Do not rush to fill silence.** A pause after your answer often brings out the real concern.
- **One concern at a time.** Ask "Is there anything else?" so you do not discover a second concern after solving the first.

### Chunk 5: Honest answers only

You may not:

- **Invent urgency** ("This price ends today.") unless it is a real, published end date (Module 1.4)
- **Promise outcomes** you cannot prove (Module 5.3)
- **Pretend to know** something you do not (Module 2.1)
- **Minimize a real limit.** If the concern is valid, say so.

A valid concern that the product cannot answer is not a failure. It is the point where you may say, "This might not be right for you" (Module 4.4).

### Chunk 6: A short fictional exchange

> **Lena:** "I'm not sure my staff will actually use it."
> **Jordan:** "That's a fair worry. Can you tell me more about what's happened with new tools in the past?"
> **Lena:** "We tried an app once. Nobody opened it after a week."
> **Jordan:** "That's helpful to know. What made it hard to use?"
> **Lena:** "Too many steps."
> **Jordan:** "Then let me show you what a booking takes here, and you tell me if it's too many steps." *(demonstrates)* "How does that compare?"
> **Lena:** "That's simpler. I'd want my front desk to try it."

Notice Jordan did not argue or promise. The concern was fear of repeating a bad experience, and showing was the answer.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name the five steps.
> 2. Why explore before responding?
> 3. What is one thing you may not do when responding?

> **Takeaway**
> Listen, acknowledge, explore, respond, and check. Keep it calm and honest.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> When you hear an objection, say it back before you answer: "So your concern is ___. Is that right?"

## Lesson 6.3: Common Objections

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can respond honestly to price, money, trust, fit, "we're fine," and "I need to think about it" objections, and handle requests that involve someone else.

*These are patterns, not scripts. Say them in your own words, and only say what is true.*

### Chunk 1: Price

**Fixed pricing changes this objection.** Because pricing is set in advance as a subscription, **there is nothing to negotiate.** You do not make up discounts or promise a different price. That makes the conversation simpler and more honest.

An open textbook says price objections are often a habit or a negotiating tactic, and that the response is to probe the concern and build the case for value, not to cut the price. With a fixed price, that is the only option anyway.

| What they say | What to do |
|---|---|
| "That's more than I expected." | Acknowledge. Ask what they expected and what they are comparing it to |
| "Can you do better on price?" | Be straight: "The price is the same for everyone. What I can do is make sure the plan fits what you need." |
| "Why is it this much?" | Explain what the plan includes and what it replaces, using their own numbers if they gave them (Module 5.2) |
| "Your competitor is cheaper." | Do not attack. Ask what is included, and compare fairly |

You may:
- **Explain the published plans** and point out a plan that fits their needs better
- **Mention official, published promotions** if they apply
- **Be clear about what the price includes and does not include,** and when it starts

You may not:
- Invent a discount or a "special" price
- Promise a price change later
- Imply the price is negotiable when it is not

### Chunk 2: Money and timing

"Cash is tight" or "not this quarter" are real. Do not argue. Ask:

> "That makes sense. Is it more about the amount, or about when the money comes in?"

Then be **honest about what the commitment is:** the cost, when billing starts, how to cancel, and anything they should know (Module 7.3). If now is not right, agree on a later date and keep it (Module 8.1).

### Chunk 3: Trust and "I've never heard of you"

This is a fair concern about a company they do not know. Answer honestly:

- **Say who we are, in plain terms.** Do not exaggerate size, history, or customer numbers.
- **Offer proof** that is real and permitted (Module 5.2)
- **Offer a way to try or verify,** if the company has one
- **Do not say "everyone knows us."**

> "Fair question. We're a newer company, and I'd rather be upfront about that. Here's what we do and how you can check it out for yourself."

### Chunk 4: Product and fit

"Will it work for my business?" is a good question. Explore what they mean, show how it works in their situation, and **say honestly what it does not do.** If it does not fit, say so (Module 4.4). Do not stretch an example (Module 5.3).

### Chunk 5: "We're fine with what we have" (already satisfied)

The textbook notes this can be a hidden objection. Do not argue. Ask with curiosity:

> "Good to hear. What do you like most about how it works now? Is there anything you wish were easier?"

Do not criticize their current tool or provider. If nothing is wrong, **respect that.** Offer to check back at a better time if they would like (Lesson 6.4).

### Chunk 6: "I need to think about it"

The open textbook says this is usually a stall that hides a worry. A sales trainer's blog (practitioner opinion, no data) says it can be a "slow no" or a "pending yes," and advises finding out which. Practical approach:

1. **Agree, kindly.** "Of course. It's a decision worth thinking about."
2. **Ask what they will be weighing.** "What are you thinking through? Is there something I could clear up now?"
3. **Offer a specific next step,** such as a short follow-up on a date, or a summary they can review.
4. **Do not pressure.** If they want time, give it.

If there is a hidden worry, a calm question often brings it out. If there is not, you have shown respect.

### Chunk 7: "I need to talk to my partner" or "my accountant"

This may be true, a polite no, or both. Respond helpfully:

- **Offer to include them:** "Would it help if I joined a short call with your partner?"
- **Offer a clear summary** they can share
- **Agree on a follow-up date**

Do not try to bypass them. Respect that the decision may be shared.

### Chunk 8: When the objection is a real complaint

Sometimes the objection points to a **genuine limitation or problem** with the product or company. Do not defend. Thank them, be honest, and **pass it to the team** so it is heard. Customer feedback helps the company improve (Module 8.2).

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. How does fixed pricing change how you respond to "Can you do better on price?"
> 2. Name two things you may not do when responding to a price objection.
> 3. What do you do if an objection points to a real limitation?

> **Takeaway**
> Respond to the real concern, be straight about price and limits, and pass real complaints on.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before your next conversation, write your honest answer to "Can you do better on price?" in one sentence. Say it aloud.

## Lesson 6.4: Respecting a No

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can recognize a clear no, leave gracefully, follow up only as permitted, and learn from objections and refusals.

### Chunk 1: A no is an answer

Not every conversation ends in a yes, and it should not. A person who says no after hearing honest information has made a decision. Respect it.

Signs of a real no:

- **A direct no:** "We're not interested."
- **A repeated no** after you have explored and responded
- **Silence after follow-ups** that you sent as agreed
- A request to **stop contacting** them

### Chunk 2: How to leave well

> "I appreciate your time, and thank you for being straight with me. If things change, I'd be glad to help. Would it be okay if I check back in a few months, or would you rather I didn't?"

- **Thank them.** Genuinely.
- **Do not guilt, argue, or sigh.**
- **Ask permission** to follow up once, and accept the answer.
- **Do not make a last-minute offer** or invent urgency.

A person who is treated well when they say no is more likely to say yes later, or to recommend you.

### Chunk 3: Honor requests to stop

If someone asks you to stop contacting them, **stop, and record it** in the approved system so no one else on the team contacts them. This is basic respect, and rules on contact exist (Module 3.4). Counsel and management will set the written policy.

### Chunk 4: Mixed signals: a soft no

A "soft no" is vague: "maybe later," "send me information," or no reply. **Ask once, kindly:**

> "It sounds like this may not be the right time. Is that fair to say?"

If they say yes, thank them and leave. If they say no, "it's just busy," agree on a clear date.

### Chunk 5: Learn from every objection

Keep a simple log of objections you hear, in plain words:

- **What was said**
- **What it turned out to be** (a question, a worry, or a no)
- **What helped**
- **What the company could do** to address it, such as clearer information or a product limit

Share patterns with your leader. If many people ask the same question, the company can answer it earlier, improve materials, or fix the product. A rep who hears the same objection ten times is holding useful information.

### Chunk 6: Your own reaction to a no

A no is not a verdict on you. After a hard one:

- **Take a minute.** Walk, breathe, or talk to a teammate.
- **Write down one thing you learned.**
- **Reset before the next contact** (Module 2.4).

If you find yourself dreading or avoiding contact, talk to your leader.

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Objection** | A concern, question, or hesitation a person raises about buying |
| **Hidden objection** | The real concern beneath what a person first says |
| **LAER** | A training model: Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, Respond |
| **Check (step)** | Asking whether an answer addressed the concern |
| **Price objection** | A concern about cost or value |
| **Money objection** | A concern about affordability or timing |
| **Source objection** | A concern about the company or the seller |
| **Stall** | A delay such as "let me think about it," which may hide a worry or a no |
| **Soft no** | A polite, vague way of declining |
| **Opt-out / stop request** | A person's request not to be contacted |
| **Objection log** | A short record of objections, what they turned out to be, and what helped |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two signs of a real no.
> 2. What do you do when someone asks you to stop contacting them?
> 3. Why keep an objection log?

> **Takeaway**
> A no is an answer. Leave well, honor requests to stop, and learn from every objection.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After each conversation, write the objection you heard in one line, and what turned out to matter.
