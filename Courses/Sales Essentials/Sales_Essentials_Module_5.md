## Lesson 5.1: Features, Benefits, and the Need They Answer

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can turn a product feature into a benefit that answers something the customer said, choose a few points instead of listing everything, and avoid presenting before you have investigated.

### Chunk 1: Presenting comes after discovery

In Module 4 you learned what the person's situation, problem, and goal are. Presenting is where you **connect what you offer to what they told you.** It is not a tour of everything the product does.

The open textbook *Selling for Success* (NSCC) makes the same point: investigate first, then show how your product addresses the stated need, because benefits work better than generic advantages when they are tailored to the buyer. It also notes that two buyers may buy the same product for different reasons.

### Chunk 2: Features and benefits

| Term | Meaning | Example (fictional scheduling tool for a salon) |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | What the product is or does | "It sends text reminders to clients." |
| **Benefit** | What that does **for this person** | "So fewer clients forget, and you don't lose a chair a day." |

A widely taught version of this idea is called **features, advantages, and benefits (FAB):** what it is, what it does, and what that means for the customer. The textbook I used does not use the FAB label, so I use the simpler pair of **feature and benefit** and add one test:

> **The "so what?" test.** After every feature you mention, ask yourself, "So what does that mean for them?" If you cannot answer in their words, leave the feature out.

### Chunk 3: Connect it to their words

A benefit is strongest when it echoes **what the customer said.** Lena said no-shows cost her a chair a day and she wants her evenings back (Module 4).

| Weak (generic) | Strong (tied to what she said) |
|---|---|
| "Our platform has automated reminders." | "You mentioned no-shows cost you a chair a day. Reminders go out automatically, so fewer clients forget, which could get you home on time more evenings." |

Notice the careful wording: **"could,"** not **"will."** You can describe what the product is designed to do and what other customers have experienced (Lesson 5.2). You cannot promise Lena's results (Lesson 5.3).

### Chunk 4: Choose a few points

A pitch that covers twenty features sounds like noise. Use this approach:

1. **List what they told you matters** (usually two or three things).
2. **Match one feature to each** and say the benefit in their terms.
3. **Stop.** Ask if it addresses what they care about.

If a feature does not connect to anything they said, **do not mention it unless they ask.** You can always say, "There's more, and I'm happy to show you anything you're curious about."

### Chunk 5: Speak to different people differently

An owner who cares about time wants to hear about hours saved. One who cares about reputation wants to hear about customer experience. One who is wary wants to hear what happens if it does not work. **Same product, different benefits,** chosen from what each person told you.

### Chunk 6: Be honest about limits

Presenting value includes saying what the product does not do:

> "This helps with reminders and bookings. It doesn't handle payroll, so you'd keep using your current tool for that."

This builds credibility (Module 1.3) and prevents refunds and complaints.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is the difference between a feature and a benefit?
> 2. What is the "so what?" test?
> 3. Why say "could" and not "will" when describing what a customer might gain?

> **Takeaway**
> Match a few features to what the customer said and say the benefit in their words. Be honest about limits.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before presenting, write two or three things the customer said matter most, and match one feature to each. Present only those.

## Lesson 5.2: Proof: Examples, Evidence, and Honest Comparisons

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can offer proof that is real and relevant, quantify value using the customer's own numbers, and compare to alternatives without misleading.

### Chunk 1: Why proof matters

Saying a product helps is easy. A small business owner has heard it before. Proof makes it believable. Good proof is:

- **Real:** it actually happened
- **Relevant:** it resembles this customer's situation
- **Specific:** details, not adjectives
- **Current:** not out of date
- **Checkable:** they could verify it if they wanted

### Chunk 2: Types of proof

| Type | What it is | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Demonstration** | Showing the product doing the thing, ideally with the customer's own situation | Staged examples that hide limits (Lesson 5.4) |
| **Customer example** | What another business did and what happened | Must be true, relevant, with permission, and not the unusual best case |
| **Documented results** | Numbers the company can substantiate | Must be accurate, current, and not overstated |
| **Trial or sample** | Letting them try it | Terms must be clear |
| **Third-party review or recognition** | What others have said or awarded | Must be genuine and properly disclosed (Lesson 5.3) |

Open textbook guidance: letting a prospect **experience the product** is one of the strongest forms of proof, since it educates, involves them, and shows performance.

### Chunk 3: Use the customer's own numbers

The same textbook suggests quantifying value, such as return on investment (ROI), using a worked example of a purchase with a cost and annual savings. That example is from a textbook for general products and uses invented figures, so do not reuse it.

For our work, the safest way to quantify is to **use the customer's numbers, not yours.** Ask, then calculate together:

> "You said you lose about one chair a day to no-shows. If a chair is worth about [your number] a day, that's roughly [their number] a week. Does that sound right?"

Then make an **if/then** statement and leave the conclusion to them:

> "If reminders cut that in half, what would that be worth to you?"

**Do not supply a figure for what they will save.** You do not know their business, and a number you invent is a claim you must be able to prove (Lesson 5.3).

### Chunk 4: Honest comparisons

Customers will compare you to other options, including doing nothing. A fair comparison:

- **Names the alternatives accurately,** including a spreadsheet, a paper book, or hiring help
- **Says what each does well,** not only what it does badly
- **Avoids attacking competitors.** A vendor article on pressure tactics and the FTC's rules both warn that false statements about a competitor can create legal risk, and even true attacks sound petty
- **Compares like with like**
- **Does not claim "best" or "only" unless you can prove it**

> "Plenty of owners start with a paper book, and if you have two or three clients a day, that works. When it gets to fifteen, it gets hard to keep track. That's where this helps."

### Chunk 5: If you do not have proof

Say so. "I don't have an example from a shop like yours, but I can show you how it works and you can judge." Or offer a trial if the company has one. Do not stretch an example to fit.

### Chunk 6: Permission and privacy

Before mentioning a customer's name or results to another customer:

- **Have their permission,** and use only what they agreed to share
- **Do not share what a customer told you in confidence** (Module 1.3)
- If you are not sure you have permission, describe the example without identifying details, or leave it out

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three qualities of good proof.
> 2. Why should you use the customer's own numbers when quantifying value?
> 3. What should you do if you have no example that fits?

> **Takeaway**
> Offer real, relevant, specific proof. Use their numbers, compare fairly, and never stretch an example.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before using a customer story, check three things: Is it true? Does it resemble this person? Do I have permission?

## Lesson 5.3: Claims, Testimonials, and Disclosure

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain the "reasonable basis" rule for claims, why a "results may vary" note does not fix an unrepresentative testimonial, and what you may and may not do about reviews.

> **Note:** This lesson summarizes public guidance from the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and law-firm summaries of a 2024 rule. It is not legal advice. State laws can add more. The lesson ends with an open item for Resona and counsel.

### Chunk 1: The basic rule

In the United States, a business that makes **objective claims** about its product, such as "reduces no-shows" or "saves five hours a week," is expected to **have a reasonable basis for the claim before making it.** The FTC's policy statement on advertising substantiation says that failing to have and rely on a reasonable basis for objective claims is an unfair and deceptive practice. The rule covers claims made in conversation by salespeople as well as in ads, because what matters is whether a claim misleads.

Key points from that policy statement:

- **Express claims:** If you say "tests prove," "studies show," or "most customers," you need at least that level of support.
- **Implied claims:** A statement can imply more than it says. You need support for what the customer will reasonably take from it.
- **No stated level of support:** Customers expect a reasonable basis. What counts depends on the type of claim, the product, the consequences of being wrong, and what experts in the field would consider reasonable.

*(The statement is from 1983. The copy I read was a 2009 printout. Check it is still current before relying on it.)*

### Chunk 2: What this means for you

Every statement about results needs a source:

| You say | You need |
|---|---|
| "It reduces no-shows." | A basis, such as documented results or testing the company can show |
| "Most customers save time." | Proof that most do, not that some did |
| "You'll see results in a week." | Proof that is typical, or do not say it |
| "Customers love it." | A real basis, such as review data, and not one or two stories |
| "The only tool that does X." | Proof that nothing else does, which is hard. Avoid it. |

If you do not have the basis, say what you **do** know: what the product does, what the plan includes, and how it works.

### Chunk 3: Testimonials and typical results

A customer story can mislead if it describes an unusual result. The FTC's Endorsement Guides FAQ (2015) says:

- **People usually read a specific testimonial as typical.** "Individual results may vary" does not change that reading.
- The business must **either have proof the result is typical, or clearly disclose what results are generally expected.**
- **Older testimonials must still be accurate.** If the product has changed, the story may no longer be fair.

The Endorsement Guides were revised in 2023. The FTC page I read confirms the Guides are not regulations, but practices that are inconsistent with them can lead to enforcement under the FTC Act.

**For you:** never present a customer's best result as what a new customer should expect. Say, "That was one owner's experience. It's not a promise of what you'll see."

### Chunk 4: Connections must be disclosed

If you recommend or review a product and you have a **relationship with the seller that could affect how people weigh your view,** such as being paid, an employee, or receiving free product, that connection must be disclosed clearly. The FTC FAQ states that employees must disclose they work for the company when endorsing its products, and that a note buried in a profile is not enough.

**For you:** you work with or for Resona. When you tell someone a product is good, that is a sales conversation and they know it. **Do not post reviews or recommendations of Resona products on public sites, forums, or social media as though you were an independent user.** If you post about your work, say who you are.

### Chunk 5: Reviews: a 2024 rule

The FTC finalized a rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, effective October 21, 2024. According to a law-firm summary of the rule, it prohibits:

- **Fake or false reviews and testimonials,** including ones that misrepresent whether the reviewer used the product or what happened
- **Paying for reviews with a particular sentiment.** Offering a discount for a review is not itself unlawful, but tying it to a positive (or negative) review is
- **Undisclosed reviews by insiders,** such as officers, managers, and employees
- **Company-controlled review sites presented as independent**
- **Suppressing negative reviews,** with narrow exceptions, and presenting selected favorable reviews as representative
- **Fake social media followers, likes, or views** used to misrepresent influence

The rule carries civil penalties per violation. The summary I read gave an amount as of 2024, and it is adjusted yearly, so do not quote a number.

**Practical rules for our team:**

- **Never write, post, or ask for a fake review.**
- **Never offer a discount, credit, or gift in exchange for a positive review.**
- **You may invite honest feedback** from real customers after real use, without telling them what to say.
- **Do not ask a customer to remove an honest negative review.** Fix the problem and tell your leader.
- **Employees and contractors do not review the company's products** as though independent.
- **Do not buy followers, likes, or views.**

### Chunk 6: When you are unsure

Ask before you say or post it. A good question to ask your leader:

> "Do we have proof for this statement, and is it okay to say it to customers?"

**Open item for Resona and counsel:** a short written list of **approved claims,** with the proof behind each one, and a written **reviews and testimonials policy** (who may post what, and how customer stories are approved and disclosed). Until then, say only what the product plainly does and what the plan includes.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What does "reasonable basis" mean for a claim?
> 2. Why does "results may vary" not fix an unusual testimonial?
> 3. Name two things our team never does about reviews.

> **Takeaway**
> Every claim needs a reasonable basis. Testimonials must be fair, connections disclosed, and reviews real.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before you say "customers usually" or "most people," ask yourself: "Can the company show that?" If not, describe a real example as one example, or leave it out.

## Lesson 5.4: Making It Easy to Understand

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain things in plain language, show instead of tell, check understanding as you go, and keep a presentation short and focused on the customer.

### Chunk 1: Clear beats clever

An owner who does not understand something will not buy it, and will rarely say so. Make it easy:

- **Short sentences, everyday words.** Say "send reminders," not "leverage automated notification workflows."
- **One idea at a time.**
- **Use their words** from discovery.
- **Explain any term** you must use, once, simply.
- **Use a concrete example** from their business, not an abstract one.

### Chunk 2: Show instead of tell

The open textbook says letting prospects **experience the product** is among the most powerful tools, since it educates them and proves performance. In practice:

- **Show the one thing that matters most to them first,** not a tour from the top menu
- **Use their situation:** "Let's say Lena books a haircut for Tuesday at 2. Here's what she sees."
- **Let them touch it,** or ask them to tell you what to try
- **Show the limits too,** so there are no surprises later (Module 7.3)

A full **demonstration structure** for Resona products is taught in the Resona Product Demo course. This lesson covers the basics that apply to any conversation.

### Chunk 3: Check understanding as you go

- "Does that make sense so far?"
- "How would that work in your shop?"
- "What questions come to mind?"
- "In your own words, what would that change for you?"

The last question is the most revealing. If they cannot say it back, you have not made it clear yet.

### Chunk 4: Keep it short

The textbook offers a rule attributed to a well-known author for slide presentations: no more than 10 slides, about 20 minutes, and a large font. Treat that as a reminder to **be brief.** For a small business owner, a presentation is often a few minutes, and a slide deck may not be needed at all.

- **Do not rely on slides or a script.** Customers want you, not a recitation.
- **Do not hide behind a screen.** Face the customer.
- **Do not bury them in a brochure.** A short, useful leave-behind is better than a stack of pages.
- **Avoid clutter, gimmicks, and distracting transitions.**

### Chunk 5: Video and phone presentations

- Treat a video call like an in-person meeting: be prepared, minimize distractions, and close with next steps (Module 3.4).
- On the phone, use simple words and check in more often, since you cannot see their face.
- In both, **avoid multitasking.** They can tell.

### Chunk 6: After you present

Do not rush to close. Ask:

> "How does this compare with what you were hoping for?"

Their answer tells you whether to move to a next step (Module 7), address a concern (Module 6), or stop.

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Feature** | What a product is or does |
| **Benefit** | What the feature does for this particular customer |
| **"So what?" test** | Asking what a feature means for the customer, and leaving it out if you cannot say |
| **Proof** | Real, relevant, specific, current, checkable support for a claim |
| **ROI** | Return on investment: what a customer gains compared with what they spend |
| **Objective claim** | A statement of fact about a product or result that can be proved or disproved |
| **Reasonable basis** | Support a business must have before making an objective claim |
| **Substantiation** | The evidence that backs a claim |
| **Testimonial** | A customer's statement about their experience with a product |
| **Typical results** | What most customers can generally expect |
| **Material connection** | A relationship with a seller that could affect how people weigh a recommendation |
| **Disclosure** | A clear statement of a connection or a limitation |
| **Demonstration** | Showing the product doing something in a customer's situation |
| **Plain language** | Clear, short, everyday words |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Give two ways to make an explanation easier to understand.
> 2. What question reveals whether the person understood?
> 3. Why is "showing" often stronger than "telling"?

> **Takeaway**
> Use plain words, show the one thing that matters most, check understanding, and keep it short.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After presenting, ask: "In your own words, what would this change for you?" Listen carefully to the answer.

## Module 5 Mastery Check

*Locked until all four lessons are complete. Suggested pass mark: 80%.*

**Q1.** Which best describes a benefit?
- A. What the product is made of
- B. A technical specification
- **C. What a feature does for this particular customer, in their terms** (correct)
- D. A list of everything the product can do

**Q2.** After you mention a feature, what should you ask yourself?
- A. "Can I say more?"
- **B. "So what does that mean for them?"** (correct)
- C. "Is this longer than my competitor's?"
- D. "Have I covered the whole menu?"

**Q3.** The customer said no-shows cost them a chair a day. Which statement is the best way to connect a feature to that?
- A. "Our platform has many features."
- B. "You'll never have a no-show again."
- **C. "Reminders go out automatically, so fewer clients forget, which could help with the no-shows you mentioned."** (correct)
- D. "Most customers love reminders."

**Q4.** What is the safest way to quantify value for a customer?
- A. Give them a savings figure that sounds impressive
- **B. Use the customer's own numbers, ask them to confirm, and let them reach the conclusion** (correct)
- C. Quote an average from a different industry
- D. Avoid any numbers

**Q5.** What does the FTC policy statement say about objective claims?
- A. Claims need no support if they are in conversation
- **B. A business should have a reasonable basis for an objective claim before making it** (correct)
- C. Claims are always acceptable if the customer asks
- D. Only written claims need support

**Q6.** A customer's testimonial describes an unusual result. What does the guidance say about a "results may vary" note?
- A. It fixes the problem
- B. It is required on every testimonial
- **C. It generally does not change how people read the testimonial; the business needs proof the result is typical or a clear statement of what is generally expected** (correct)
- D. It is only needed in writing

**Q7.** A teammate suggests offering a discount to customers who leave a five-star review. What is the right response?
- A. Agree; it is a standard tactic
- B. Offer it only to loyal customers
- **C. Decline; tying a reward to a positive review is a problem, and inviting honest feedback is fine** (correct)
- D. Offer it but don't write it down

**Q8.** You are asked "Is this the only tool that does this?" and you do not know. What do you say?
- A. "Yes, it's the only one."
- B. "Definitely, nobody else does."
- **C. "I don't know every other option. Here's what this does, and you can compare."** (correct)
- D. "Don't worry about other tools."
