# Sales Essentials
## Module 4: Discovery

**Prerequisite:** Sales Essentials Modules 1 to 3

## Lesson 4.1: Why Questions Beat Pitches

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain what discovery is for, describe what you are trying to learn, and say why investigating before presenting makes a conversation better for both people.

### Chunk 1: What discovery is

**Discovery is the part of the conversation where you learn whether, and how, what you offer fits this person's situation.** It happens after the opening (Module 3) and before you present anything (Module 5).

It has two purposes:

- **For the customer:** to describe their situation and realize what matters to them, often for the first time out loud
- **For you:** to learn enough to give honest, relevant help, or to learn that you cannot

### Chunk 2: The problem with pitching first

Compare two moments with the same owner, a fictional hair-salon owner named Lena:

> **Pitch first:**
> "Our platform handles scheduling, reminders, reporting, and payments in one place."
> *Lena's silent reaction:* "I already use a paper book and it works fine. What does this have to do with me?"
>
> **Question first:**
> "What's the most frustrating part of running the schedule right now?"
> *Lena's reaction:* "Honestly? No-shows. I lose about a chair a day when someone doesn't come."

In the first version Lena has to decide, alone, whether any of the features matter. In the second she names the problem herself, and now everything you say later has something to attach to.

### Chunk 3: Sellers who investigate first

An open textbook on selling (*Selling for Success*, Nova Scotia Community College) teaches a four-part flow for a call: **opening, investigating, demonstrating capability, and obtaining commitment.** It says the investigation has to come before the presentation, "or the pitch will miss." The textbook builds that flow on the SPIN research described in Lesson 4.2, so treat it as a well-organized teaching model, not as independent proof. I looked for independent studies of questioning in small-sale selling and did not find a source I could rely on, so this course teaches discovery on the strength of practice and common sense: **it is hard to help someone you have not listened to.**

### Chunk 4: What you are trying to learn

For a small business owner, discovery usually covers six things. You do not need all of them in one conversation.

| You want to learn | Example question |
|---|---|
| **Situation:** how things work today | "Walk me through how a typical week goes." |
| **Problem:** what is not working | "What's the most frustrating part of that?" |
| **Impact:** what it costs them (time, money, stress, customers) | "What does that cost you when it happens?" |
| **Goal:** what better would look like | "If that were fixed, what would be different?" |
| **Constraints:** what limits their choices | "Is there anything that would make a change hard right now?" |
| **Decision:** who decides, and how | "Who else would weigh in on something like this?" |

### Chunk 5: Honest discovery

Discovery is not a tool for finding a lever to pull. Two rules:

- **Ask because you want to know,** not to steer. If you already decided what the answer should be, you are pitching with question marks.
- **Be ready for answers that point away from your product.** "My real problem is hiring" is useful, true, and may mean you are not the right help. Say so (Lesson 4.4).

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What are the two purposes of discovery?
> 2. In the Lena example, why does the question-first version work better?
> 3. Name three things you try to learn in discovery.

> **Takeaway**
> Learn the person's situation, problem, and goal before you present. Ask because you want to know.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before presenting anything, ask yourself: "Could I explain this person's biggest frustration in their own words?" If not, ask more.

## Lesson 4.2: A Question Toolkit

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can use open, closed, and probing questions on purpose, apply the four SPIN question types with their limits, and avoid leading, loaded, or interrogating questions.

### Chunk 1: Three kinds of question

| Type | What it is | Good for | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Cannot be answered with yes or no; invites explanation | Getting the story | "How do you handle appointment changes now?" |
| **Closed** | Short answer: yes, no, a number | Confirming facts; narrowing | "Do you have more than one location?" |
| **Probing** | Follows up on something they just said | Going deeper; clarifying | "You said Mondays are chaotic. What happens?" |

Rule of thumb: **start open, probe to understand, then close to confirm.** Do not use closed questions to steer ("You'd like to save time, wouldn't you?"). They are not discovery.

### Chunk 2: The SPIN framework

A well-known framework for questions was developed by Neil Rackham and colleagues from observations of sales calls. It sorts questions into four kinds. The open textbook gives examples:

| Question type | Purpose | Example (fictional salon) |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Learn basic facts about their current setup | "How many stylists work here, and how do they book clients?" |
| **Problem** | Help them name a difficulty or dissatisfaction | "What's hardest about handling the booking book?" |
| **Implication** | Explore what the problem leads to | "When someone no-shows, what happens to the rest of that day?" |
| **Need-payoff** | Let them say what a solution would be worth | "If you could fill that gap, what would that mean for you?" |

### Chunk 3: Using SPIN well, and its limits

**What it gets right:**

- It moves from facts, to difficulties, to consequences, to benefits, and lets **the customer** state the value.
- It encourages you to ask more and tell less.

**What to watch** (from the open textbook's cautions, plus my own):

- **Ask Situation questions sparingly.** Too many bore people. **Do the research first** (Module 2.2) so you do not ask what you could have looked up.
- **Do not assume the person sees the problem just because you do.** Ask, do not tell.
- **Investigate before presenting benefits.** Otherwise the pitch will miss.
- **Implication questions must be honest.** They are for helping a person see real consequences, **not for creating fear or inflating a problem.** If you are exaggerating to build pressure, stop. That is the pressure tactic from Module 1.4.
- **The research behind SPIN has limits.** The best-known source is the framework's own training company. It describes the original research as concerning **major, high-value sales, not small ones,** and it gives few statistical details, so its claims cannot be checked. Treat SPIN as **a useful way to organize questions,** not as proven science, and not as a script.
- **It is a toolkit, not a ritual.** A conversation with a small business owner often takes two or three minutes of questions, not a full SPIN sequence.

### Chunk 4: Questions that go wrong

| Problem | Example | Better |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** (suggests the answer) | "Wouldn't you agree your schedule is a mess?" | "How would you describe your schedule right now?" |
| **Loaded** (assumes something) | "When did you start losing money on no-shows?" | "How much of an issue are no-shows?" |
| **Stacked** (several at once) | "How many stylists, and what software, and what's your budget?" | One at a time |
| **Interrogating** (rapid-fire) | A string of questions with no response in between | Pause, acknowledge, then ask |
| **Closed when you needed open** | "Do you have scheduling problems?" | "What scheduling challenges come up?" |
| **Too personal or not your business** | "What do you earn?" | Ask about the business need, not private finances |

### Chunk 5: Questions that open people up

Useful, natural prompts:

- "Tell me more about that."
- "What does a good week look like? And a bad one?"
- "What have you tried before?" (And: "What happened?")
- "What's the one thing you'd change if you could?"
- "What would make this worth looking at, and what would make it not worth your time?"
- "Is there something I should be asking that I haven't?"

### Chunk 6: A short fictional exchange

> **Jordan:** "Lena, thanks for the time. Before I say anything about us, can I ask a few questions about how you run the schedule?"
> **Lena:** "Sure."
> **Jordan:** "How do clients book with you today?"
> **Lena:** "Phone, text, walk-ins. I write it in a book."
> **Jordan:** "What's the most frustrating part of that?"
> **Lena:** "No-shows. I lose a chair a day, sometimes."
> **Jordan:** "That sounds painful. What happens to the rest of that day when it happens?"
> **Lena:** "I end up scrambling, and I'm there late."
> **Jordan:** "If you didn't have to scramble, what would that give you?"
> **Lena:** "My evenings back."

Notice that Jordan did not mention any product. Lena named the problem, its effect, and what she wants.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What is the difference between an open and a probing question?
> 2. Name the four SPIN question types.
> 3. Rewrite this to remove the leading: "Wouldn't you agree your schedule is a mess?"

> **Takeaway**
> Start open, probe, then confirm. Use SPIN as a way to organize questions, ask honestly, and do not manufacture pressure.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Prepare three open questions for the next conversation, and write them where you can see them.

## Lesson 4.3: Listening for the Real Need

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can use paraphrasing, summarizing, and clarifying to confirm you understood, and tell the difference between what someone says and what they need.

### Chunk 1: What people say and what they need

The first answer is often not the whole answer.

| What they say | What may be underneath |
|---|---|
| "It's too expensive." | "I'm not sure it will work for me." |
| "We're fine." | "I haven't thought about it." or "Not now." |
| "I need to talk to my partner." | A real second decision-maker, or a polite no |
| "We tried something like this." | "It went badly, and I'm wary." |
| "Can you send me information?" | Interest, or a polite way to end the call |

You find out by **asking kindly** ("What happened last time?"), not by assuming.

### Chunk 2: Active listening skills

A counselor-written overview of active listening lists these core skills, which transfer well to sales:

| Skill | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Paraphrasing** | Restate what you heard in your own words | "So no-shows are your biggest drain." |
| **Summarizing** | Pull together the key points | "Let me check I've got it: bookings come by phone and text, no-shows cost you a chair a day, and you'd like your evenings back." |
| **Reflecting** | Repeat the essence, including the feeling, without parroting | "It sounds like that's stressful." |
| **Clarifying** | Ask about anything vague | "When you say 'a mess,' what does that look like?" |
| **Open questions and encouragers** | Keep them talking | "Tell me more." "And then?" |
| **Attending to tone and body language** | Notice how it is said, as a clue (Module 3.3) | "I noticed you slowed down there. Is that a sore spot?" |

*(The overview is from a counselor's website and draws on counseling and communication research. That research is mostly about counseling and healthcare, not sales. The skills are widely taught, and it is reasonable to use them here.)*

### Chunk 3: Say it back

Saying back what you heard does three things: it shows you are listening, it corrects misunderstandings early, and it often prompts the person to add something important. Make it a habit:

> "Let me make sure I have this right. [Their situation.] [Their problem.] [What they want.] Did I get that right?"

If they say "not exactly," that is useful. Ask, "What did I miss?"

### Chunk 4: Habits that get in the way

- **Planning your answer while they talk.** You miss the second half.
- **Finishing their sentences.** It feels presumptuous.
- **Jumping to a solution** the moment you hear a problem.
- **Correcting them** when something they say is not quite right. Note it and move on, unless it matters.
- **Filling silences.** A pause often leads to the most honest part of the answer.
- **Checking a phone or the room.**

### Chunk 5: Take notes, and say you are

Write down what matters: their words, the problem, what they want, and any worries. Say, "Mind if I jot that down?" Notes show respect and make your follow-up specific (Module 8.2). **Record only business-relevant, non-sensitive information** (Module 2.2).

### Chunk 6: Listen for what limits their choices

Besides the problem, listen for:

- **Time:** "We can't take anything on until after the holidays."
- **Money in general terms:** "Cash is tight right now." (You do not negotiate price, since it is fixed, but you can be honest about what the plan costs and when it starts. Module 6.3.)
- **People:** "My staff hates new tools."
- **History:** "We got burned by a contract."
- **Decision:** "I run everything past my accountant."

These tell you how to present honestly and what to avoid promising.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Give an example of how a first answer may not be the whole answer.
> 2. What are paraphrasing and summarizing?
> 3. Why take notes, and what should you not write down?

> **Takeaway**
> Listen for the need behind the words, say it back, and ask kindly when something is unclear.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After the person finishes describing their situation, pause, then summarize in two sentences and ask, "Did I get that right?"

## Lesson 4.4: When to Stop Asking

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can tell when you have learned enough, check whether the product truly fits, move from discovery to presenting with permission, and end politely when it does not fit.

### Chunk 1: When you have enough

You do not need to ask forever. Stop asking when you can honestly say:

- I understand **their situation and the problem they care about**
- I know **what they would like to change**
- I know **whether this could fit,** and what to avoid promising
- I know **who decides and when**

Over-asking turns a conversation into an interrogation and uses their time.

### Chunk 2: A quick fit check

Use what you know from Module 2.1 (who the product helps and who it does not). Ask yourself:

| Check | Question to yourself |
|---|---|
| **A real problem?** | Did they describe something that matters to them? |
| **A fit?** | Does what we offer actually address it? |
| **The right person?** | Are they the one who decides, or will someone else weigh in? |
| **Timing?** | Is this a now thing, a later thing, or never? |
| **Able and willing to commit?** | The plan is a fixed-price subscription. Does that work for them in general terms, without you probing their finances? |
| **Reasonable expectations?** | Do they expect something we cannot honestly deliver? |

A venture investor's article argues that spotting poor-fit customers early saves time and builds trust. It is opinion with no data, and written about larger business sales, so use the idea only.

### Chunk 3: Moving from discovery to presenting

Before you present, **summarize and ask permission:**

> "Based on what you've told me, it sounds like [situation, problem, goal]. Would it be useful if I showed you how some owners handle that, and you tell me whether it fits?"

This keeps the person in control and signals that what comes next is tied to **their** words (Module 5.1).

### Chunk 4: When it is not a fit

Saying "this is probably not right for you" is a strength. It earns trust and saves everyone time. Do it kindly and clearly:

> "Based on what you've told me, I don't think this is the right fit. Your biggest challenge is hiring, and that isn't something we help with. I'd rather tell you now than waste your time. If something changes, I'm happy to talk again."

Where you can, **point to a better resource,** such as a type of provider or a general source of help, **without recommending a specific company unless Resona has an approved referral.** Do not leave a poor-fit customer with a sale they will regret. It leads to refunds, complaints, and a bad name.

### Chunk 5: When it is a fit but not now

- Agree on **a specific next step with a date** (Module 7).
- **Note** what they said they need to see before deciding.
- Do not push. Follow up as agreed (Module 8.1).

### Chunk 6: Record what you learned

Write a short note right after: their situation, the problem in their words, what they want, constraints, who decides, and the next step. Business-relevant, non-sensitive, and kept in the approved system (Module 2.2).

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Discovery** | The part of a conversation where you learn whether and how your product fits the person's situation |
| **Open question** | A question that invites explanation |
| **Closed question** | A question with a short answer such as yes, no, or a number |
| **Probing question** | A follow-up that digs into something the person just said |
| **SPIN** | A question framework: Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff |
| **Leading question** | A question that suggests the answer you want |
| **Loaded question** | A question that assumes something not yet established |
| **Paraphrasing** | Restating what you heard in your own words |
| **Summarizing** | Pulling together the key points to check understanding |
| **Reflecting** | Repeating the essence of what was said, including the feeling |
| **Underlying need** | The real concern beneath the first thing a person says |
| **Fit check** | A quick review of whether the product suits the person |
| **Poor fit** | A situation where the product is unlikely to serve the person well |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two signs you have asked enough.
> 2. What do you say before moving from discovery to presenting?
> 3. Why can telling someone "this isn't the right fit" be a strength?

> **Takeaway**
> Ask until you understand, check the fit honestly, ask permission to present, and be willing to say "not a fit."

> **When you're working with a customer**
> End each discovery with a two-sentence summary and a question: "Did I get that right? May I show you how this might help?"
