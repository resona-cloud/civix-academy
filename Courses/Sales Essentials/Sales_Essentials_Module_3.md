# Sales Essentials
## Module 3: Opening and Rapport

**Audience:** Everyone at Resona who talks with people about buying
**Prerequisite:** Sales Essentials Modules 1 and 2

## Lesson 3.1: The First Thirty Seconds

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can open a conversation by saying who you are, why you are there, and asking permission to continue, and you can name common openers that backfire.

### Chunk 1: What an opening has to do

A small business owner is usually in the middle of something when you appear. In the first moments they are silently asking three questions:

1. **Who are you?**
2. **What do you want?**
3. **How long will this take?**

A good opening answers all three quickly, honestly, and politely, then **asks permission to continue.** It does not try to sell anything yet.

### Chunk 2: A simple structure

| Part | What you say | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| **Name** | Your name and company | "Hi, I'm Jordan with Resona." |
| **Reason** | Why you are reaching out, in one sentence | "I'm talking with local shop owners about how they handle their appointment scheduling." |
| **Why them (optional)** | One fact from your prep (Module 2.2) | "I saw you opened a second chair this year." |
| **Permission and time** | Ask whether now works and say how long | "Do you have two minutes, or is there a better time?" |

That is it. The owner decides what happens next.

### Chunk 3: Details that matter

A Canadian open textbook on selling (*Selling for Success*, NSCC, 2023) lists habits that apply to every kind of opening:

- **Get the name right.** Spelling and pronunciation count. Errors signal that you did not care enough to check.
- **"Don't be interesting; be interested."** The textbook quotes this advice. Questions and follow-ups show real interest.
- **Have a one-minute description of what you do ready,** so you can answer "what is this about?" without rambling.

For phone openings, the same textbook suggests:

- **State your name and purpose within about 20 seconds,** and use a prepared script.
- **Ask "Is this a good time?"** instead of a question that invites an easy no, such as "Did I catch you at a bad time?"
- **Skip "How are you today?"** as a cold opener. It sounds like a script. Something like "Thanks for taking my call" is better.
- **Avoid long explanations.** Busy decision-makers view cold calls as interruptions.

### Chunk 4: Openers that backfire

| Opener | Why it backfires |
|---|---|
| "I'm not trying to sell you anything" (when you are) | Misleading, and people hear it as a sales line |
| "How are you today?" read in a flat voice | Signals a script |
| A long description of the company | Makes the person wait to learn why you are there |
| "Is the owner in?" with no name or reason | Gatekeepers hear it as a pitch and the owner hears it as an interruption |
| An unrelated compliment you do not mean | Fake rapport (Lesson 3.2) |
| Launching into features | Skips the person completely |

### Chunk 5: If the answer is "not now"

"Not now" is a complete, fair answer. Say:

> "No problem at all. When would be better, or would you rather I send a short note first?"

Then **do exactly what you offered.** A person who is treated well when they say "not now" is much more likely to say yes later (Module 8.1).

### Chunk 6: Practice the opener

Write yours in two sentences. Say it aloud. Time it. If it takes more than about twenty seconds, cut it.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name the three things an owner wants to know in the first moments.
> 2. What is the difference between "Is this a good time?" and "Did I catch you at a bad time?"
> 3. What should you do when someone says "not now"?

> **Takeaway**
> Say who you are, why you are here, and ask permission. Then let the person decide.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Write your opener, read it aloud, and cut every word the person does not need in the first twenty seconds.

## Lesson 3.2: Rapport Without Fakery

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can describe how rapport is built from real interest, listening, and respect, and say why technique-driven tricks tend to backfire.

### Chunk 1: What rapport is

**Rapport is the sense that the other person is comfortable with you and that you are on the same side.** It is not charm and it is not a trick. It grows when a person feels **heard, respected, and not rushed.**

In Module 1 you saw that high self-orientation lowers trust. Rapport works the same way: the less you appear to be working an angle, the more people relax.

### Chunk 2: What builds it

| Habit | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Genuine interest** | Asking about their business and caring about the answer |
| **Listening fully** | Not interrupting; not planning your reply while they talk |
| **Using their name** | Spelled and pronounced correctly |
| **Matching their pace** | Brief with a busy owner, more relaxed with someone who wants to talk |
| **Respecting their time** | Saying how long you need, and keeping to it |
| **Finding real common ground** | Noticing something you actually share, such as a neighborhood or a shared challenge |
| **Warmth and humor, naturally** | A smile, a light comment, but never at their expense |
| **Following through** | Doing what you said (Module 1.3) |

### Chunk 3: Listening that shows

People can tell whether you are listening. Show it in simple ways:

- **Pause before you answer.** A one-second pause tells the person you thought about what they said.
- **Say back the key point.** "So the biggest headache is the Monday schedule changes." (Module 4.3)
- **Ask a follow-up** that proves you heard the detail.
- **Put the phone away** and face the person.
- **Take a brief note,** and say so: "Mind if I jot that down?"

### Chunk 4: A caution about mirroring and other techniques

You will meet advice to copy the other person's posture, gestures, and speech to create rapport. Some of it is harmless. A former FBI behavioral analyst writing in *Psychology Today* describes people naturally mirroring each other when they are comfortable. **That article is a blog post based on anecdotes. It cites no studies, and a number of its claims have no support.** Treat it as a description of what comfort sometimes looks like, not as a method.

Why not to rely on technique:

- **Forced mimicry looks odd,** and people notice.
- **It shifts your attention to yourself,** not the customer, which raises self-orientation.
- **Gestures are ambiguous.** Crossed arms can mean discomfort, or a cold room, or habit.

**If you want one simple rule:** be natural, be interested, and let your body language follow your attention.

### Chunk 5: Small talk, in measure

- **Short, relevant, and optional.** A comment about something visible and positive ("Great storefront") is fine. Move on if they are busy.
- **Follow their lead.** If they want to chat, chat for a minute. If they are watching the door, get to the point.
- **Avoid sensitive subjects** such as politics, religion, and personal matters.
- **Never flatter falsely.** If you cannot say it honestly, do not say it.

### Chunk 6: Respect is the foundation

Respect shows in small things: arriving when you said, not interrupting their work with customers, not touching their things, and accepting a no gracefully (Module 6.4). Rapport that rests on respect survives disagreement. Rapport that rests on technique does not.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three habits that build rapport.
> 2. Why can forced mirroring backfire?
> 3. What is one way to show you are listening?

> **Takeaway**
> Real interest, full listening, and respect build rapport. Tricks tend to backfire.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> In your next conversation, count how many times you say back something the person said. Aim for at least two.

## Lesson 3.3: Reading Signals

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can recognize common signs of interest, hesitation, and "not now," and respond by checking in instead of guessing.

### Chunk 1: Signals are clues, not answers

People show how they feel through words, tone, pace, and body language. These are **clues.** They can be misread, so the best practice is to **notice, then check.**

The *Psychology Today* article from Lesson 3.2 lists cues such as leaning in, open posture, and nodding as signs of comfort, and crossed arms or objects placed between people as signs of distance. **Its evidence is anecdotal, and the author's own examples show many exceptions.** Use the cues only to prompt a question.

### Chunk 2: A signal guide

| What you notice | It might mean | A good response |
|---|---|---|
| Asks questions about details or how it works | Interest | Answer simply, then ask what matters most to them |
| Leans in, nods, says "go on" | Engaged | Keep going, and invite their view |
| Short answers, looking away, checking the time | Rushed or uninterested | Say: "I can see you're busy. Should I come back, or give you the one-minute version?" |
| Interrupts you | Impatient or has a concern | Stop. Ask what they were about to say |
| Silence | Thinking, or lost | Wait a few seconds, then: "What's on your mind?" |
| Long list of "what ifs" | Worry | Ask which worry matters most (Module 6) |
| Polite but vague ("sounds interesting") | Possibly no, softly | Ask: "What would you want to see to know if it's worth a closer look?" |
| Glances at customers, employees, or the door | Their attention is elsewhere | Offer to pause or follow up |
| Warmth and humor | Comfort | Stay natural; do not push |

### Chunk 3: Check in out loud

You do not have to guess. Short check-ins are respectful and effective:

- "Is this useful so far?"
- "Am I explaining this clearly?"
- "What questions come to mind?"
- "Would it help to stop here and pick it up another time?"

A check-in gives the person an easy way to steer you. Many people will say "I'm just busy right now," and you will learn it is not a no.

### Chunk 4: Hear the real "no"

A polite owner may not say no directly. Phrases that often mean "no, thank you":

- "Send me some information" (and nothing more)
- "Let me think about it" with no question asked
- "Maybe next quarter" with no reason
- Not returning calls after a clear interest

Do not treat these as tricks. Treat them as honest signals. **Ask once, kindly, and respect the answer:** "It sounds like this may not be the right time or fit. Is that fair to say?" (Module 6.4).

### Chunk 5: Take your own signals seriously

Notice yourself too. If you feel the urge to speed up, talk more, or push, that is a signal to slow down and ask a question.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Why is it risky to rely on body language alone?
> 2. Give an example of a check-in question.
> 3. What are two phrases that often mean a polite no?

> **Takeaway**
> Notice signals, then check in out loud. Treat a soft no as a real answer.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> If you are unsure how a person feels, say so kindly: "I can't tell if this is useful. Can you tell me honestly?"

## Lesson 3.4: Different Settings

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can adapt your opening to in-person, phone, video, and written contact, and describe the basic rules about unsolicited calls and texts.

### Chunk 1: In person

Walking into someone's business means you are a guest in their workplace.

- **Read the room first.** Is it busy? A line of customers or a rush is the wrong time. Offer to come back.
- **Never interrupt a customer interaction** to talk to the owner.
- **Ask for the right person politely:** "Is the owner or manager available for two minutes?" Respect the answer.
- **Introduce yourself briefly** with your name, company, and purpose (Lesson 3.1).
- **Leave something simple behind** if they ask: a card or short note with your contact details and what to expect.
- **Notice the setting:** space, hours, how things work. It helps you ask better questions later (Module 2.2).
- **Be easy to leave.** Thank them and go when they signal they are done.

### Chunk 2: Phone

From the same open textbook:

- Be **brief and direct,** since rapport is harder without visual cues
- **State your name and purpose within about 20 seconds**
- **Ask whether it is a good time** and offer to call back
- **Avoid long explanations**

Add a few practical points: stand or sit upright (it affects your voice), speak a bit slower than feels natural, and have your goal and notes in front of you (Module 2.3).

### Chunk 3: Video

A marketing agency's guide to video sales calls (it sells inbound-marketing training, and the post dates from 2020) gives these practical points, which are common sense for any video call:

- **Test your setup first:** camera, microphone, and the meeting link
- **Face your light source.** A window behind you leaves you in shadow
- **Sit upright and frame yourself so your face is visible**
- **Smile and look at the camera sometimes;** on video, a neutral face can look serious
- **Explain how to use the tool** if the person is unsure
- **Use their name** and invite them into the conversation
- **Address distractions politely** and keep to the time you said

The post claims close rates are higher with cameras on but cites no source, so I did not use that claim. **Do not require a customer to turn on their camera.** Offering is fine, and some people cannot or prefer not to.

### Chunk 4: Email, text, and social

Per the open textbook, written first contact is a first impression:

- **Proofread,** and use the person's name correctly
- **Personalize** every message, so it does not look like a template
- **Keep it short,** with one clear ask
- **Persistence is fine; pestering is not.** Space out follow-ups and stop when asked (Module 8.1)
- **On social networks,** personalize connection requests, contribute value before pitching, and remember that anything you post can be seen by customers

### Chunk 5: Rules about unsolicited calls and texts

Rules about phone calls, text messages, and email to people you have not spoken with before exist, and they can be strict. **This is a legal area, and what follows is a general orientation, not legal advice. The rules vary and change, and I did not read the primary sources.**

What I found:

- A 2024 law-firm update says the **Federal Trade Commission's Telemarketing Sales Rule** was amended so that **its ban on false or misleading statements now applies to business-to-business calls** as well as consumer calls. This matters because most Resona customers are businesses. **The simple rule: everything you say on a sales call must be true.** (Module 1.4)
- A compliance-software vendor's page states that **business-to-business marketing is not exempt from all rules.** It says that sending marketing calls or texts to a mobile number using an **automatic dialing system** generally requires the recipient's **prior express written consent,** whether the recipient is a business or a consumer. *(The vendor sells compliance products, and the page did not say who qualifies, how the rules apply to small business owners' personal mobile numbers, or what the penalties are.)*
- **The details of Do Not Call rules for business numbers were not clear in what I read.**

**Our practice, until management sets a written rule:**

- **Use only company-approved tools and templates** for calls, texts, and emails
- **Do not send marketing texts** to a mobile number unless the person has given consent through an approved process
- **Do not use automated dialing or messaging tools** unless they are company-approved
- **Honor "stop," "unsubscribe," and "do not contact" immediately,** and record it
- **Ask your leader** whenever you are unsure

*(An open item for management and counsel: a written contact policy covering calls, texts, and email, including consent and opt-outs.)*

### Chunk 6: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Opening** | The first moments of a conversation, when you say who you are and why you are there |
| **Permission-based opening** | An opening that asks whether the person has time to talk |
| **Rapport** | The comfortable sense that the other person is on your side |
| **Active listening** | Listening fully and showing you heard, by pausing, saying back, and following up |
| **Mirroring** | Matching another person's gestures or speech, whether naturally or on purpose |
| **Check-in** | A short question that asks how the person is doing with the conversation |
| **Soft no** | A polite, vague way of declining |
| **Gatekeeper** | A person who controls access to the decision-maker |
| **Consent (for contact)** | A person's agreement to be contacted in a particular way |
| **Opt-out** | A person's request to stop being contacted |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. What should you check before approaching an owner in person?
> 2. What is the rule that applies to everything you say on a sales call?
> 3. Name two things we do about calls, texts, and email until management sets a written rule.

> **Takeaway**
> Adapt to the setting, respect the person's time, and follow the contact rules. Everything you say must be true.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before each contact, ask: "Is this the right moment and the right channel for this person?" If you are not sure, ask first.
