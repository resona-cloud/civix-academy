# Sales Essentials
## Module 7: Closing and Commitment

**Audience:** Everyone at Resona who talks with people about buying
**Prerequisite:** Sales Essentials Modules 1 to 6
**Lessons:** 7.1 Closing as a Natural Next Step · 7.2 Reading Readiness and Trial Questions · 7.3 Clear Terms and Consumer Protections · 7.4 After the Yes
**Who "the customer" is:** Individual decision-makers, mostly small business owners and their staff.
**Tone:** Plain language. Honest selling. Fictional examples. Product-neutral.
**Pricing note:** Pricing is set in advance as a subscription. Reps explain published plans and any official promotions. They do not negotiate or invent prices.

## Lesson 7.1: Closing as a Natural Next Step

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain closing as a normal, honest next step, and ask for a commitment clearly and without pressure.

### Chunk 1: Closing starts at the beginning

A closing is not a trick at the end. It is **the point where you and the customer agree on what happens next.** That may be signing up, a second meeting, a trial, or "not now."

An open sales textbook (*Selling for Success*, NSCC) makes the same point: closing is a series of steps that starts when you qualify the customer (Module 4.4), not a single moment. Good questions, honest fit checks, and clear value (Modules 4 and 5) do most of the work. If those were done well, asking is simple.

### Chunk 2: Why you should ask

The textbook says customers expect to be asked once you have earned the right through trust and value. A person who needs a decision from you cannot decide if you never say what the decision is. Not asking is not polite; it leaves the customer guessing.

Asking can be calm:

| Situation | A calm ask (fictional) |
|---|---|
| Ready to sign up | "Would you like to get started with the plan we talked about?" |
| Needs a step first | "The next step is a short setup call. Would Thursday or Friday work?" |
| Unsure | "Where would you like to leave this? We can go ahead, talk again, or stop here." |

### Chunk 3: Choices that are honest

The textbook lists several closing styles: a direct request, a summary of benefits, an assumptive close, an alternative-choice close, and a compliment close. Here is how to use them honestly in this company:

| Style | What it is | Use it honestly |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct ask** | "Would you like to go ahead?" | Always fine |
| **Summary** | Recap what they said mattered, then ask | Use their words, not yours (Module 5.1) |
| **Alternative choice** | Offers two options that both say yes | **Only between real, published plans or real dates.** Never between "yes" and "yes" when "no" is a real answer |
| **Assumptive** | Speaks as if the decision is made | **Use with caution.** Do not assume when the customer has not said yes. Ask, do not tell |
| **Compliment** | Praises the customer's choice | Only if sincere. Never as a lever |

Do not use any style to rush, trap, or hide a "no."

### Chunk 4: What closing is not

From Module 1.4, these stay off limits:

- **Invented deadlines** ("This price ends today.")
- **Guilt, fear, or pressure** to force a decision
- **Ignoring a clear no**
- **Offering a special price.** Pricing is fixed. You offer published plans and official promotions only.

The textbook says the same: closing should not involve high-pressure tactics, trickery, or manipulation.

### Chunk 5: Keep it brief

The textbook advises keeping the request short and focused on the customer's biggest problem. After you ask, **stop talking.** Silence lets the person think. Filling it often leads to talking them out of their own decision.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. In one sentence, what is closing?
> 2. When is an alternative-choice close honest?
> 3. What should you do right after you ask?

> **Takeaway**
> Closing is agreeing on the next step. Ask clearly, keep it honest, and then stop talking.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before a conversation, write the one sentence you will use to ask for the next step. Say it aloud.

## Lesson 7.2: Reading Readiness and Trial Questions

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can notice signs of readiness, use a trial question to check where a customer stands, and respond to the answer.

### Chunk 1: Signs of readiness

The textbook lists moments that suggest a customer may be ready:

- They **ask questions** about how it works, timing, or setup
- They **share more about their business**, or react warmly
- They **ask about next steps,** delivery, or start dates
- They **bring in someone else** who will use it or decide
- **Right after you resolve an objection** (Module 6), when they have what they need to decide

Treat these as clues, not proof. Check before you assume (Module 3.3 made the same point about body language).

### Chunk 2: Signs they are not ready

- **Short, flat answers** or "looks good so far" with no energy
- **"Let me think about it,"** or needing to involve others (Module 6.3)
- **Concerns that have not been answered**
- **Silence** after you ask

A vendor article (Rework, a sales-software company) groups responses into strong positive, soft positive, neutral, and objection signals, and says neutral ones usually mean an unanswered concern. That article cites no research, so treat the grouping as practical advice.

### Chunk 3: Trial questions

A **trial close** asks for an opinion, not a decision. It is a low-pressure way to see where the person stands. The textbook says it lowers pressure "for both of you."

| Type | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| **Opinion** | "What stands out to you so far?" |
| **Scale** | "On a scale of 1 to 10, how well does this fit what you need?" |
| **Priority** | "What's most important to you in choosing?" |
| **Concern check** | "Is there anything that would hold you back?" |
| **Timing** | "What's your timeline for putting something in place?" |

Follow-up is what matters. If someone says "6," ask: **"What would make it a 9?"** Their answer is the next conversation.

### Chunk 4: Reading the answer

| What you hear | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Positive and specific** | Move to a clear ask (Lesson 7.1) |
| **Positive but vague** | Probe: "What would be the next step for you?" |
| **Negative or hesitant** | The textbook calls this a signal to change your approach, not to push. Explore the concern (Module 6.2) |
| **No answer** | Slow down. Ask what is on their mind |

Two cautions from the vendor article, which match common sense: **do not ask so often that it feels like pressure,** and **do not move past a negative answer.**

### Chunk 5: Do not close too early

The textbook warns that closing too early can make a customer disengage. If you have not asked about their needs (Module 4) or shown value (Module 5), your ask will feel pushy because it is premature. Go back and ask another question.

### Chunk 6: A short fictional exchange

> **Jordan:** "What stands out to you so far?"
> **Marco:** "The scheduling part. That's where I lose time."
> **Jordan:** "On a scale of 1 to 10, how well does this cover that?"
> **Marco:** "Maybe a 7."
> **Jordan:** "What would make it a 9?"
> **Marco:** "I'd want to see how my techs would use it on their phones."
> **Jordan:** "Fair. Would it help if I showed you that now?"

Jordan did not close. Jordan found the real gap, and the next step became obvious.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name two signs of readiness and one sign of not being ready.
> 2. What is the difference between a trial question and a closing question?
> 3. A customer answers "6." What do you ask?

> **Takeaway**
> Look for signs, check with a trial question, and follow the answer. Do not rush.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Ask one trial question midway through each conversation. Write down what you learned.

## Lesson 7.3: Clear Terms and Consumer Protections

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can explain terms plainly before a customer commits, describe how cancellation works, and know where legal rules may apply and when to ask for help.

*This lesson is general education. It is not legal advice. The company's counsel decides how rules apply to Resona. When in doubt, ask your leader.*

### Chunk 1: The standard we hold

Whatever the law requires, the standard in this company is simple: **no surprises.** Before a customer says yes, they should be able to say back to you:

| What they should know | Plain example |
|---|---|
| **What they are buying** | "The plan includes X and Y, and does not include Z." |
| **What it costs** | "It's [published price] per [month/year], billed on [date]." |
| **When billing starts** | "You'll be charged on [date], not before." |
| **How it renews** | "It renews each [period] unless you cancel." |
| **How to cancel** | "You can cancel by [published method]. Here's what happens to your data and billing." |
| **Who to contact** | "If something goes wrong, contact [published channel]." |

Use the company's actual terms. If you do not know one, say so and find out before the customer commits. **Never guess about terms** (Module 2.1).

### Chunk 2: Why subscriptions need extra care

Subscriptions repeat. A customer who does not clearly understand the renewal and cancellation terms can feel trapped, and regulators have focused on exactly this.

- **ROSCA** (the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act, 2010) is a federal law about online sales with recurring charges. According to a May 2026 law-firm summary, it requires sellers to **clearly disclose material terms before collecting billing information, get express informed consent before charging, and offer a simple way to stop the charges.**
- The FTC's **2024 "click-to-cancel" rule** was **vacated by a federal appeals court in 2025** on procedural grounds. According to the same summary, the FTC began a new rulemaking in March 2026. **No new final rule had been issued** as of that article. The FTC continues to bring cases under existing laws.
- The summary **does not say whether ROSCA applies to sales to businesses.** That is a question for counsel.

You do not need to memorize these. You need to know that **clear terms, clear consent, and easy cancellation are what regulators look for,** and they are also what keeps customers.

### Chunk 3: The Cooling-Off Rule, and why it probably does not apply here

The FTC's **Cooling-Off Rule** gives buyers three business days to cancel certain sales made away from the seller's place of business, such as at the buyer's home, workplace, or a temporary location like a hotel or fairground. Highlights, from FTC consumer guidance and the rule's definitions:

- It covers **consumer** goods and services, defined as bought **mainly for personal, family, or household purposes.** Goods and services **not** mainly for those purposes are not covered. The rule's definition adds that **courses of instruction or training are covered whatever the reason someone takes them.**
- It has dollar thresholds: **$25 or more at the buyer's home and $130 or more at other locations.**
- It does not cover sales made **entirely by phone, mail, or online,** or sales made after the buyer negotiated at the seller's permanent place of business.
- When it applies, the seller must **tell the buyer about the cancel right, provide two copies of a cancellation form, and give a dated contract or receipt.** The seller must refund within 10 days of a valid cancellation.

**What this means for us.** Our customers are small business owners buying for business use, so the rule **generally would not cover most of our sales.** But three cautions:

1. **"Generally" is not "never."** Some offerings, such as training, are covered whatever the reason. Some sole proprietors blur personal and business use.
2. **State laws differ** and may cover more. The FTC says the rule does not replace state cooling-off laws (rule text and a 2013 FTC notice).
3. **The company decides policy, not individual reps.** If a customer asks about a cancellation right you do not know, do not guess. Say: "Good question. Let me get you the exact terms."

### Chunk 4: Practical habits

- **Put terms in writing** and send them, so nothing rests on memory.
- **Make cancellation easy to find** and describe it honestly. Never hint that it is hard.
- **Never rush a signature.** If someone wants time, give it (Module 6.3).
- **Never hide terms in fine print** or skip over them.
- **Confirm in their words:** "Just to be sure, you understand it renews each month and you can cancel at any time by [method]. Is that right?"
- **Record what you told them** (Module 8.2), keeping to non-sensitive information (Module 2.2).

### Chunk 5: When to stop and ask

Stop and ask your leader (who may involve counsel) when:

- A customer asks for **special terms, a refund outside the published policy, or a different contract**
- A customer says they are buying **mainly for personal or household use**
- A customer asks **how a law applies** to them
- A customer **says they did not understand** the terms after signing
- You are **not sure what you told them** is accurate

None of this is a reason to avoid selling. It is why we can sell with confidence.

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name four things a customer should be able to say back to you before they commit.
> 2. Why does the Cooling-Off Rule probably not cover most of our sales? Name one reason that is still uncertain.
> 3. A customer asks what the law says about cancelling. What do you do?

> **Takeaway**
> No surprises. Explain terms plainly, make cancellation easy, and ask for help when the law is unclear.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> Before asking for a yes, say aloud the price, billing date, renewal, and how to cancel. If you cannot say one, find out first.

## Lesson 7.4: After the Yes

> **Objective**
> By the end of this lesson you can confirm a commitment, set honest expectations, and support a customer through their first week.

### Chunk 1: The yes is the start

The textbook describes closing as part of an ongoing relationship, not an end. A customer who signs up and then hears nothing may regret it. A customer who is welcomed well is more likely to stay, use the product, and recommend you.

### Chunk 2: Confirm clearly

Within a short time after the yes:

1. **Thank them.** Sincerely.
2. **Recap what they chose:** the plan, price, billing date, and renewal (Lesson 7.3).
3. **State what happens next,** with dates when possible: "You'll get an email today. Setup is [step]. I'll check in on [date]."
4. **Say how to reach help.**
5. **Send it in writing.**

### Chunk 3: Set honest expectations

Overpromising now creates the complaint later. Review what you said in the conversation:

- **Do not add promises** to keep the customer happy
- **Do correct anything you said that was unclear.** Fix it now.
- **Be honest about the first weeks:** "Most people take a little while to settle in. Here's what's normal."

### Chunk 4: The first week

| When | Helpful action |
|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Confirmation sent; they know the next step |
| **First few days** | Check that setup works; answer questions |
| **End of week one** | A short check-in: "How is it going? Anything confusing?" |

The goal is for the customer to **get early value.** If they hit a problem, handle it quickly, and pass product problems to the right team (Module 6.3).

### Chunk 5: Do not use the sale to ask for more

Right after a yes, do **not** immediately push extras, referrals, or reviews. Let them experience the product first. Referrals and reviews come later, and with care (Module 8.3).

### Chunk 6: If they change their mind

Some customers cancel. Treat it as you would a no (Module 6.4):

- **Thank them** and be gracious
- **Explain the cancellation process honestly** and follow the published terms
- **Ask, once and kindly,** whether you could have done something better. Record the answer.
- **Never** guilt, delay, or make it hard

### Chunk 7: Glossary for this module

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Closing** | Agreeing with the customer on the next step, which may be a sale |
| **Trial close** | A question that asks for an opinion, not a decision, to see where the customer stands |
| **Buying signal** | A sign a customer may be ready to decide |
| **Direct ask** | A plain request such as "Would you like to go ahead?" |
| **Alternative-choice close** | Offering two real options, both of which move forward |
| **Assumptive close** | Speaking as if the decision were made; use with caution |
| **Material terms** | The important facts a customer needs to decide, such as price, renewal, and cancellation |
| **ROSCA** | A federal law about online sales with recurring charges, requiring clear disclosure, consent, and simple cancellation |
| **Cooling-Off Rule** | An FTC rule giving buyers three business days to cancel some sales made away from the seller's place of business |
| **Consumer goods or services** | Items bought mainly for personal, family, or household use |
| **Cancellation process** | The published steps a customer follows to end a subscription |
| **Confirmation** | A recap, in writing, of what the customer chose and what happens next |

> **Retrieval Practice**
> 1. Name three things to include in a confirmation.
> 2. Why not ask for a referral right after the yes?
> 3. What do you do if a customer cancels?

> **Takeaway**
> The yes starts the relationship. Confirm clearly, set honest expectations, and help in week one.

> **When you're working with a customer**
> After every yes, send a short written recap the same day.
